# ELECTENG MAGIC — Level 4/5 of the PCB Grimoire: Folk Knowledge, Goblin Docs, and the Silica Spider

## TL;DR
- **The folk layer is real but uneven:** some veteran heuristics are genuine physics in disguise (via-stitching λ/20, copper thieving for warp, decoupling geometry), some are situational (guard traces, teardrops, ferrite beads), and a few are actively debunked by measurement — the 20-H rule is the clearest cargo-cult, shown by Shim & Hubing to *increase* edge radiation by ~3.6 dB at the TM10/TM01 resonant modes.
- **Your fastest wins are documentary and architectural:** exploit the "goblin docs" (TI SLUP power seminars, Jim Williams' LT AN47, Analog Devices AN-1368, Würth Trilogy of Magnetics) and Shenzhen DFM culture (JLCPCB basic/extended parts, the LCSC rotation trap, ≤5-designs-free panel rule); and accept that a coreless PCB-stator motor's defining problems are **very low inductance** (forcing high FOC switching frequency or added series L) and **heat extraction** from a thermally dead FR-4 stator (~0.4 W/mK).
- **The silica-spider-as-PCB is buildable at the flex-hinge / pop-up-laminate scale (Harvard PC-MEMS lineage), not as a rigid FR-4 skeleton under repeated leg impact** — FR-4's brittleness, low thermal conductivity, and ED-copper trace fatigue (4–11% elongation vs 20–45% for rolled-annealed) make dynamic structural loading a measure-don't-believe zone.

## Key Findings

1. **The single hardest-debunked rule you should stop using is the 20-H rule.** Shim & Hubing's *20-H Rule Modeling and Measurements* (Proc. IEEE Int. Symp. EMC, pp. 939–942) concludes verbatim that "the 20-H rule DOES NOT decrease radiated emissions from a board. On the contrary, both the analytic and measured results indicated that boards implementing the 20-H rule exhibit slightly higher emissions at resonant frequencies" — measured at least 3.6 dB more at the TM10 mode.
2. **Most "grounding" folklore collapses into one correct principle** (Rick Hartley, Bruce Archambeault, Henry Ott): control the return current's loop area by keeping signals one dielectric from an unbroken reference plane. Split planes, star grounds, and moats are usually solutions to problems created by bad stackups.
3. **Vendor app-note literature is the highest-value free engineering education available**, and it exists because vendors profit when their silicon works in your design. Read it as physics + a sales incentive, not neutral truth.
4. **The Shenzhen ecosystem publishes its DFM constraints openly**; the binding limits for your 140-board run are the basic/extended parts split, the LCSC part-rotation problem, and the ≤5-different-designs-per-panel-free / ≤10-max rule at JLCPCB.
5. **A micro PCB-stator QDD motor is real (arXiv 2509.23561) but the honest numbers are humbling:** 158 mN·m stall, 23.4 mN·m *continuous* stall, 60% peak efficiency, 148 °C at 8 W — and that required a 48-layer IC-substrate HDI stator you cannot buy at JLCPCB-class fabs.
6. **Drone ESCs are speed controllers, not servo controllers.** BLHeli_S/BLHeli_32 six-step commutation cannot hold position or torque at stall; you need FOC (moteus, ODrive, SimpleFOC, VESC) with current sensing.
7. **For a walking/impacting robot, vibration/shock fatigue is governed by Steinberg's method** — keep the board's first natural frequency well above the excitation band and ≥2× above the chassis mode.

## Details

### TARGET 1 — Folk practices: the folk rule, the mechanism, the verdict

**The 20-H rule.** *Folk rule:* pull the power plane back from the board edge by 20× the plane-to-plane spacing (H) to contain fringing fields and cut edge radiation. *Mechanism:* the claim is that fringing fields wrap the edge and radiate; setting the power plane back supposedly confines them. *Reality:* Shim & Hubing (UMR-Rolla / Clemson CVEL) built test boards with and without the setback, drove them with an RF source, and measured with near-field probes. Result: near fields are more contained, but total radiated power is **at least 3.6 dB *higher*** at the resonant frequencies corresponding to the TM10 and TM01 cavity modes (EMAP5 modeling confirmed, with measured differences ~7 dB at the TM10 mode). A separate IEEE MoM study found the setback *generates common-mode current* in the ground plane. **Verdict: CARGO-CULT** for radiation reduction. The one defensible use is that pulling *all* copper (including signals) back from the edge, plus edge-stitching to a solid ground, does help — but that is edge-guarding, not the 20-H rule.

**The 3-W rule.** *Folk rule:* keep trace centers ≥3× the trace width apart to limit crosstalk. *Mechanism:* crosstalk falls with separation; 3-W corresponds to roughly −20 to −26 dB coupling in typical microstrip. *Reality:* as Altium/Lee Ritchey note, crosstalk is fundamentally a function of the **loop area and the trace-to-reference-plane height (h)**, not trace width. The physically correct rule is spacing referenced to dielectric height, not width. **Verdict: SITUATIONAL** — a usable proxy when h tracks with width, but the real variable is height above the plane.

**Via-stitching spacing (λ/10, λ/20).** *Folk rule:* stitch ground vias around planes/edges at λ/10 to λ/20 of the highest frequency. *Mechanism:* the plane cavity behaves like a "waveguide beyond cutoff"; vias short the cavity modes and prevent edge slot-antenna radiation. *Reality:* this is genuine — the EDN analysis (*Via spacing on high-performance PCBs*) shows λ/8 gives good isolation and λ/20 gives the ultimate (>120 dB) isolation, matching waveguide-cutoff analysis. Note this is a **maximum** spacing (EMI) that must coexist with a **minimum** fabrication spacing; at high frequency the two can collide. **Verdict: GENUINE** (with the caveat that internal-plane stitching often matters more than a perfect edge grid, per the MDPI via-fencing susceptibility study which found edge fencing did little because coupling wasn't at the edges).

**Guard traces / guard rings.** *Folk rule:* a grounded trace between an aggressor and victim kills crosstalk. *Mechanism:* a guard trace only helps if it is stitched to the reference plane with vias at ≤λ/10 intervals so it cannot itself resonate. *Reality:* an un-stitched or poorly stitched guard trace becomes a resonant coupling structure and can make crosstalk *worse*; on a good stackup with a solid adjacent plane, guard traces add little. Guard rings are genuinely valuable for **DC leakage/femtoamp** guarding (electrometer inputs) and for isolating sensitive analog — a different mechanism from RF crosstalk. **Verdict: SITUATIONAL** — genuine for leakage guarding, often cargo-cult for high-speed crosstalk on a properly-referenced board.

**Capacitor value ladder (100 nF + 10 nF + 1 nF...).** *Folk rule:* stagger decoupling cap values by decades to cover more frequency. *Mechanism:* each cap has a self-resonant frequency (SRF); the ladder supposedly extends the low-impedance band. *Reality:* mixing decade values creates **anti-resonances** — parallel resonances between one cap's inductance and another's capacitance — producing sharp impedance *peaks* between the SRFs. Modern practice (Bogatin, Archambeault, Larry Smith's PDN work) is to use **many identical capacitors of one or two values**, minimizing mounting inductance (loop area) rather than chasing decade coverage. **Verdict: CARGO-CULT** in its strong "one of each decade" form; the mechanism that matters is mounting inductance and count.

**Copper thieving / balanced copper.** *Folk rule:* add non-functional copper (dots/hatch) to empty areas and balance copper top-to-bottom. *Mechanism:* uneven copper density causes uneven etch rates and, critically, **differential thermal expansion during reflow → warp/bow/twist**; IPC-6012 limits bow-and-twist to 0.75% for SMT boards. *Reality:* genuine and important, especially for large/multilayer/HDI boards. **Verdict: GENUINE** (this is the one you should exploit deliberately on your spider's large structural panels).

**Teardrops.** *Folk rule:* add teardrop fillets at trace-to-pad/via junctions. *Mechanism:* originally to tolerate **drill wander** so a slightly-off drill still lands on copper; secondarily to reduce stress concentration and etch-related neck-down. *Reality:* the drill-wander and mechanical stress-relief benefits are real and well-attested (originated with less-precise drilling equipment). Reliability claims like "reduce trace cracking by up to 30% in automotive vibration" appear in fab blogs (AllPCB) — treat as vendor rule-of-thumb, not audited data. **Verdict: GENUINE for mechanical/manufacturing robustness** (directly relevant to your impacting robot), aesthetic-only for signal integrity on most boards.

**Thermal reliefs / snowflake pads.** *Folk rule:* connect pads to planes with spoke "snowflakes" so they solder well. *Mechanism:* full copper connection wicks reflow heat into the plane, causing cold joints and tombstoning; spokes throttle heat. *Reality:* genuine tradeoff — thermal relief improves solderability/reworkability but adds resistance and inductance and reduces current capacity and heat-sinking. For a **motor-driver or high-current path you want solid connections**; for hand-soldered/reworkable grounds you want relief. **Verdict: GENUINE tradeoff.**

**Ground stitching around board edges / edge guarding.** Genuine when it connects two ground planes into a Faraday-cage edge (per the Gajjar/LinkedIn and speedingedge analyses), but of limited benefit if the dominant coupling is not at the edges (MDPI). **Verdict: SITUATIONAL.**

**Chassis / mounting-hole grounding.** See the "genuinely disputed" section — this is a real expert disagreement, not settled folklore.

**One-point ("star") ground.** *Folk rule:* tie all grounds to a single star point to avoid ground loops. *Mechanism:* valid at low frequency (audio, DC precision) where a single node prevents circulating currents. *Reality:* at RF, a star ground's inductance is fatal and return current *wants* to flow directly under its signal; Hartley/Ott advocate a **solid plane** above ~100 kHz. **Verdict: SITUATIONAL** — genuine below ~50–100 kHz, cargo-cult at high speed.

**Ferrite bead misuse.** *Folk rule:* drop a ferrite bead in a power rail (or between grounds) to "block noise." *Mechanism:* a bead is a frequency-dependent lossy inductor that becomes resistive only above ~20–30 MHz. *Reality:* Analog Devices **AN-1368 "Ferrite Bead Demystified"** (Eco & Limjoco), using the TDK MPZ1608S101A, documents the classic failure: "Peaking occurs when the resonant frequency of a low-pass filter network, formed by the ferrite bead inductance and the high-Q decoupling capacitance, is below the crossover frequency of the bead. The resulting filter is underdamped ... it may amplify ripple and noise" — and these LC resonances typically fall at **0.1–10 MHz**, right around DC-DC switching frequencies, so an undamped bead+cap can *amplify* the very switching noise you meant to filter. Separately, DC bias collapses the bead's impedance — use a bead at **~20% of its rated current** because inductance can drop to ~15–30% of nominal near rating. Beads *between analog and digital grounds* (as in the criticized Adafruit ADS1115 layout) are usually a mistake. **Verdict: SITUATIONAL, frequently misused.** Add series damping (a small resistor or a lossy bead) to kill the peak.

**Common-mode chokes.** Genuine and physics-based for common-mode currents on cable pairs/differential lines; misused when placed on a single-ended line expecting differential-mode filtering. **Verdict: GENUINE when applied to true common-mode paths.**

### TARGET 2 — The "goblin docs": vendor application literature worth reading

Why it exists: vendors profit when their part designs in successfully, so they subsidize world-class free education. Read it as physics plus a sales incentive.

- **Texas Instruments — the Unitrode/Power Supply Design Seminar (SLUP) series.** The crown jewels: **Lloyd Dixon's Magnetics Design Handbook** (SLUP132, and per-section SLUP123 etc.), **"Designing Planar Magnetics"** (SLUP198), **"Eddy Current Losses in Transformer Windings"** (SLUP197) — directly relevant to your PCB-stator eddy/proximity losses — and layout notes like SLUP230 ("Constructing Your Power Supply — Layout Considerations"). TI also publishes reference designs (TI Designs), the free **Power Stage Designer** tool, and DRV-series motor-driver datasheets/app notes.
- **Analog Devices / Linear Technology — Jim Williams' legendary app notes.** **AN47 "High Speed Amplifier Techniques"** (Aug 1991) is the canonical masterpiece; **AN25 "Switching Regulators for Poets," AN43 "Bridge Circuits," AN65** backlight, **AN79** settling-time, **AN94** slew-rate. Williams wrote 62 LT + 21 National app notes (>1,600 pages, per his Wikipedia bibliography). Also **AN-1368** (ferrite beads), and the broader ADI/LT catalog (Bob Pease lineage at National: "Troubleshooting Analog Circuits").
- **Würth Elektronik — "Trilogy of Magnetics."** An 800+-page design guide (5th ed., 826 pages, authored by Rall, Zenkner, Gerfer, Brander) covering EMI filter, SMPS, and RF magnetics including skin/proximity/AC-loss chapters and the free **REDEXPERT** loss simulator — highly relevant to your motor-frequency copper losses.
- **Others worth naming:** Maxim/ADI tutorials, Microchip AN series (motor control), Silicon Labs, ST (STSPIN, motor-control app notes), Infineon (gate-drive, MOSFET), and passive-vendor technical papers — Murata's decoupling/noise-suppression manuals, TDK ("All Ferrite Beads Are Not Created Equal," Chris Burket), KEMET and Vishay flex-crack/derating papers.
- **How to exploit them:** evaluation-board **gerbers and BOMs are published** — clone the vendor's own layout for their chip (they debugged it for you), and start from reference designs rather than datasheet "typical application" schematics.

### TARGET 3 — Shenzhen / Chinese manufacturing knowledge-sharing

- **Open DFM guides:** JLCPCB, PCBWay, and Elecrow publish capability tables and DFM articles openly; treat them as **vendor rules-of-thumb, not audited data**.
- **Basic vs Extended parts (JLCPCB assembly):** "Basic" parts are pre-loaded on the pick-and-place (no per-part feeder setup fee); "Extended" parts incur a feeder/loading fee. That fee was **cut from $3 to $1.5 per unique component type** (per TechOverflow, updated 2025-12-19: "Extended components: 1.5$ per component type (feeder loading fee — previously 3$)"). Note the trap: **Standard PCBA charges the $1.5 fee on ALL parts** (basic and extended), whereas Economic PCBA lets Basic parts load free — so for a BOM dominated by extended parts, Standard can actually be cheaper. The binding library is **JLCPCB's assembly parts list, which is a subset of the full LCSC catalog** — a part on LCSC is not necessarily assemblable.
- **The LCSC part-rotation problem:** EDA tools and LCSC's tape-and-reel orientation define "0°" differently, so components frequently import rotated 90/180°. This is the single most common assembly defect; you must verify rotation in JLCPCB's placement preview / Parts Placement Editor and often maintain a rotation-correction table (well-known KiCad→JLCPCB export issue).
- **公板 / "gongban" public reference designs and Huaqiangbei:** the Shenzhen ecosystem circulates open reference layouts and a dense component market; **LCSC/EasyEDA open libraries change design choices** — engineers pick parts by what is Basic/in-stock rather than by first-principles selection.
- **Capability/cost thresholds that trigger price jumps** (order-of-magnitude, verify at quote time): layer count (2→4 is cheap; jumps at 6, 8+), min trace/space (down to ~3.5/3.5 mil standard, finer = HDI premium), controlled impedance (adds cost + coupon), HDI/blind-buried vias (large premium), heavy copper (2 oz+), castellated holes, and aluminum/copper-core (MCPCB) for thermal.
- **Panelization rules (critical for your 140-board run):** JLCPCB treats boards with *different* traces/silk/soldermask as **"different designs"** even if connected. You can put **up to ~5 different designs free** inside one board outline if you separate them yourself with silk lines (self-cut); **beyond 5 it's charged, max 10 designs** per board. V-cut requires ≥70×70 mm panels and straight full-length cuts. Mixed designs must share stackup, thickness, copper weight, and finish. "Panel by JLC" is recommended above 50 pcs.

### TARGET 4 — Multi-variant prototype strategy (~140 boards, mixed versions)

- **The design-panel / "MPW analogue":** put several variants inside one outline. Given JLCPCB's ≤5-free / ≤10-max rule, the clean strategy is either (a) group variants into panels of ≤5 designs each with self-cut silk separators, or (b) pay the multi-design surcharge for a proper V-scored/tab-routed panel with up to 10 variants, sharing one stackup.
- **DOE discipline:** treat the sweep as a **factorial or Taguchi orthogonal-array** experiment. Identify factors (e.g., trace width, decoupling scheme, via count, winding geometry, gate-drive resistor), assign each variant a defined combination, and ensure every result is attributable. A fractional-factorial or Taguchi L-array lets you span many factors in ~140 units without full combinatorial explosion.
- **Traceability:** put a **human-readable revision + a machine-readable ID on silkscreen** — serial number, QR/DataMatrix, and/or **board-ID resistor straps or DIP/solder-jumper codes** that firmware can read at boot so each board self-reports its variant. Add **impedance test coupons and a controlled-impedance/microsection coupon** on the panel edge.
- **Hardware version control:** KiCad + git for source; use **KiCad assembly variants** (or Altium variants) to manage BOM/DNP differences from one base design; tag each fab run. Keep a bring-up log keyed to the board serial: record pass/fail, measured Rphase/Lphase, thermal, and failure mode per unit so the DOE is analyzable.

### TARGET 5 — PCB-stator axial-flux motors (deep)

**The reference datapoint (arXiv 2509.23561, Wang, Han, Gupta, Pathak, Zhang — CMU Robotics Institute + Microbot Motor):** a two-stator/one-rotor AFIR-AFPM, 19 mm OD, 7 mm ID, 5 mm thick, 5 pole-pairs, 9 virtual slots, **48 PCB layers** (4× 12-layer HDI IC-substrate modules), 0.06 mm/layer, 0.36 mm trace width, **≥45% copper fill** (a record; vs <35% for wound micro-stators and ~6 layers for typical PCB motors), 0.25 mm air gap, 24–48 V. **Crucially it retains an iron core** (soft-magnetic composite) — not coreless — because at low robot speeds iron loss is negligible and the core roughly doubles flux. Measured (Table II): Kt = 32 mN·m/A, back-EMF 9.48 V pk at 3000 rpm, terminal R = 4.7 Ω, terminal L = 3 mH, **stall torque 158 mN·m, continuous stall torque 23.4 mN·m, peak efficiency only 60%**, 148 °C at 8 W stall dissipation, stall torque density 5.32 N·m/cm³ (hot). The paper reports "a 61.7% increase in stall torque density" over a Maxon ECX-class reference — but at much lower efficiency (60% vs 83%).

**Design equations / scaling laws (U. Kentucky SPARK lab — Chulaee, Heins, Ionel — plus the arXiv paper):**
- **Torque scales with the cube of outer diameter** (AFPM) vs square for radial — the core reason axial-flux wins in a flat robot joint.
- Torque constant Kt rises with copper fill, turns, layer count, and pole count; but **I²R loss** rises with resistance, which rises as copper fill falls — the fundamental micro-motor squeeze (wire <50 µm can't be wound at good fill, hence PCB windings).
- Key ratio **kd = Di/Do** (inner/outer diameter) is a primary optimization variable; inner turns contribute less torque (shorter active length, worse angle) — the SPARK work notes inner tracks generate "non-contributing torque."
- A real *coreless* PCB AFPM (SPARK, HVAC-scale) hit **96% efficiency, 19 N·m at 2100 rpm** — coreless works at scale/speed, not at 19 mm/stall.

**Winding geometry (SPARK ECCE paper + coil-pattern studies):** radial and concentric windings give highest induced voltage/torque; **radial had ~20% less phase resistance than concentric**; radial winding also had the **lowest THD** in back-EMF (best for FOC). Wave windings limit eddy-current paths to the radial portion (magnets don't cover end-turns); simply widening wave-winding cross-section cut copper loss ~17%. Spiral windings suffer more eddy/proximity loss across many turns/layers.

**Eddy/proximity losses:** the dominant coreless-design concern is **eddy currents in wide copper** at motor frequency; mitigations are narrower/segmented traces, avoiding large continuous copper in the flux path, and the SPARK loss-mitigation procedure (analytical + 3D FEA within PCB manufacturing limits).

**Thermal (the real limit):** FR-4 conducts only ~0.4 W/mK, so a coreless stator is a thermal dead-end. Options: **thermal vias, dedicated non-electrical thermal copper**, insulated-metal substrate, heavy copper. **US Patent 11,342,813 (assignee Blue Canyon Technologies; inventors Steg, Carton, Peters; granted May 2022)** claims a specific mechanism: **radially-oriented copper "thermal traces," electrically isolated from the coils, with open (non-looping) distal ends, feeding an outer annular heat-spreader ring bolted to chassis** — the non-looping narrow geometry (portions <0.1 in / 2.5 mm wide) deliberately **suppresses eddy currents** that a solid heat-spreader plane would incur. Note the patent's context is a spacecraft reaction-wheel motor in vacuum (no convection), but the in-plane thermal-trace idea transfers directly to your stators.

**The low-inductance problem (your defining electrical constraint):** coreless PCB stators have very low L. Low L means current ramps extremely fast, so a fixed-frequency PWM produces **large current ripple and RMS heating for a given torque**, and current-sense/commutation timing errors cause big current spikes (documented on the ST STM32 motor-control forum for a ~0.1 mH motor). Fixes: **(1) raise PWM/switching frequency** (SimpleFOC's ~20 kHz default is too low; you may need 50–100 kHz+); **(2) add discrete series inductance** to each phase to make the controller happy (explicitly recommended in the build-its-in-progress coreless motor work); **(3) use a low-inductance-capable driver.** US patents in this space (12143057, 12519362) describe adaptive switching-frequency schemes precisely for sub-conventional-inductance windings ("≥10× lower than a comparable slot-wound motor").

**Drivers:** TI DRV-series (DRV8301/8323, MCF8316) are the industry reference; **moteus (mjbots)** and **ODrive** are tuned for precise robotics FOC; **VESC** targets scooters/vehicles; **SimpleFOC** is the accessible open library but its low-side current sensing is PWM-frequency-constrained. Ben Katz's open MIT mini-cheetah controller (MS thesis, dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/118671) is the canonical small robotics BLDC servo driver.

**Encoders:** Bugeja's IR-through-FR4 gray-code optical encoder is a clever PCB-native option; more robust are magnetic encoders (**AS5047, AS5600, MT6701, TLE5012**) and **inductive/eddy-current PCB-coil position sensors** (Renesas/Microchip inductive position sensing, PCB-coil resolvers) — the latter are attractive because the sensing coil is just more PCB copper, and can be designed to reject the motor's stray field.

**Practitioners/vendors:** Carl Bugeja (open PCB motors + flexible PCB actuators — his 6-pole 4-layer stator, 16 mm OD, 15 Ω phase, hit 85 °C on 4 V); ziteh/pcb-motor and sabanekko3/pcb_stator (open repos); **Infinitum** and **ECM PCB Stator Technology** (commercial; ECM claims zero cogging, ~half the weight/axial length, linear torque/current for robotics).

### TARGET 6 — Drone-component reuse for a legged robot

- **ESCs are speed controllers, not servo controllers.** BLHeli_S (16-bit, open, v16.7) and BLHeli_32 (32-bit, closed, licensing terminated 2024) use **six-step trapezoidal commutation** and are built to spin props one direction fast — they **cannot hold a commanded position or produce controlled torque at/near stall**, and their sensorless commutation loses sync at zero speed. **AM32** (open successor) adds experimental FOC and better low-speed behavior but is still a throttle-driven ESC at heart.
- **Why FOC matters for legs:** a legged joint spends much of its life near stall producing torque; that demands **field-oriented control with phase-current sensing and rotor-angle feedback** — the moteus/ODrive/DRV domain, not the drone-ESC domain.
- **Quasi-direct-drive philosophy (MIT Cheetah lineage; Seok/Wang/Wensing "Proprioceptive Actuator Design in the MIT Cheetah," IEEE T-RO 2017; Katz "A Low-Cost Modular Actuator"):** pair a torque-dense large-gap-radius BLDC with a low gear ratio (<10:1) and FOC for backdrivable, high-bandwidth force control and impact mitigation — this is your architecture, and it is why your PCB-stator torque numbers matter so much.
- **Thermal derating at stall:** flight ESCs assume airflow and low duty at any one throttle; a stalled leg is continuous DC-ish current in fixed phases → localized heating. Budget continuous *stall* current, not peak.
- **What transfers cleanly:** IMUs/flight controllers (with caveats below), power distribution boards, connectors, and telemetry. **LiPo sizing differs fundamentally:** flight is a roughly steady high-power draw; legged locomotion is **impulsive** — leg-impact current spikes cause **voltage sag/brownout** that can reset controllers. Size cells for peak impulse current and add bulk capacitance at the driver; consider Li-ion for energy density if peak C-rate allows.

### TARGET 7 — Vibration, shock, mechanical reliability (beyond MLCC cracking)

- **Steinberg's method is the governing framework** (Dave Steinberg, *Vibration Analysis for Electronic Equipment*). Key rules:
  - **3σ rule:** in Gaussian random vibration, design to peak stress = 3× RMS (covers 99.73% of peaks); damage is accumulated across the 1σ/2σ/3σ bands (68.3%/27.1%/4.33% time fractions) via Miner's rule (the "3-band" method).
  - **Octave rule / frequency separation:** keep the PCB's fundamental natural frequency out of the excitation band; and keep the **ratio of PCB natural frequency to its supporting structure's natural frequency ≥ 2** (the crafe.net Steinberg-applicability study states this explicitly) to avoid dynamic coupling that multiplies the Q's and magnifies response. A conservative target pushes this ratio higher still.
  - **Displacement limit:** Steinberg's fatigue-limit equation gives the maximum allowable 3σ relative displacement at the board center for 20 million cycles as a function of board dimensions and component length/position — components at the **center of the board have the shortest fatigue life; near edges is safer**; larger components should not lie parallel to the short edge.
- **Solder-joint fatigue:** Coffin-Manson / Engelmaier models for thermal-cycle fatigue; thermal cycling and vibration **interact** (a joint fatigued by thermal cycling fails sooner under vibration).
- **Staking/corner-bonding:** IPC-A-610 and **NASA-STD-8739** series govern adhesive staking of tall/heavy components and connectors; corner-bond your motor drivers, connectors, and any tall caps. Conformal coating adds damping but complicates rework.
- **Connectors:** fretting corrosion under vibration — **gold-on-gold** contacts resist fretting far better than tin; strain-relieve all wires.
- **Oscillators:** quartz crystals are **microphonic** and can shift/fail under shock; a packaged MEMS/oscillator can be more robust in an impacting robot.
- **MEMS IMU vibration sensitivity/aliasing:** MEMS gyros/accels alias structural vibration into the control band, which is exactly why drone builders **soft-mount flight controllers** — carry this over and low-pass/soft-mount your IMU.

### TARGET 8 — PCB as structure / the silica spider

**FR-4 mechanical properties (Isola 370HR datasheet, Rev. B, and IPC-4101):**
- Flexural modulus ~**25.8 GPa (warp) / 21.9 GPa (fill)** — note ~18% weave anisotropy; generic FR-4 ~18–24 GPa.
- Flexural strength ~**620 / 531 MPa**; tensile ~**385 / 245 MPa** (large warp/fill anisotropy — orient board grain deliberately in a load-bearing member).
- **Tg 180 °C** for this high-Tg grade; standard/general-purpose FR-4 is **130–140 °C**.
- **CTE: in-plane ~13–14 ppm/°C; Z-axis 45 ppm/°C below Tg, 230 ppm/°C above Tg** (the ~5× jump above Tg tears plated through-holes — a reliability cliff).
- **Moisture absorption ~0.15%**; **thermal conductivity ~0.4 W/mK** (the reason PCB structures can't shed motor heat).

**Copper trace fatigue under flex:** rolled-annealed (RA) copper elongates ~**20–45%** before break vs electrodeposited (ED) copper's ~**4–11%**; ED's columnar grain cracks at grain boundaries under bending. **Use RA copper in any flexing zone.** IPC-4562 grades foil; dynamic-flex fatigue life of RA foil exceeds **10⁵ cycles**; ED is only allowed for static bends. A 2025 Nanomaterials study measured triple-rolled/annealed foil flexural-fatigue life ≈ 8×10⁴ cycles (optimal at cube texture ~30–45%, grain size ~40–60 µm).

**Flex / rigid-flex bend radius rules (IPC-2223):** minimum bend radius = multiplier × total flex stack thickness:
- Single-layer **static: 6×**; double-layer static ~10×; multilayer static ~10–20×.
- Single-layer **dynamic: 100×**; double-layer dynamic ~150×; multilayer dynamic up to ~200× (multilayer dynamic discouraged; dynamic flex generally limited to ≤2 layers).
- Keep **stiffeners and the rigid-to-flex transition out of the bend zone** (stress concentration); use **bookbinder** construction for tighter multilayer bends; coverlay adds to the thickness used in the calc.

**PCB compliant mechanisms / pop-up laminate robotics (Harvard Wyss / Rob Wood lineage):** the **Pop-Up Book MEMS / PC-MEMS** process laminates ~18–23 layers (carbon fiber, Kapton, brass, adhesive, ceramic) with laser-cut **flexural hinges** that fold a planar sheet into a 3D machine. Per Baisch, Ozcan, Goldberg, Ithier & Wood, "High speed locomotion for a quadrupedal microrobot" (*Int. J. Robotics Research*, 2014), this produced **HAMR** — a **1.27 g walking robot with >60 joints reaching 0.44 m/s (10.1 body-lengths/s)** — and the piezo-driven **RoboBee** (120 Hz wingbeat). This is the proven paradigm for "PCB-as-structure-and-mechanism" — but at insect scale with **compliant flexures**, not rigid load-bearing panels.

**Honest verdict on the silica spider:** using PCB laminate as **compliant hinges and integrated wiring/structure at small scale is proven** (Harvard PC-MEMS, Bugeja's flexible-PCB legs and actuators). Using rigid FR-4 panels as the **primary load-bearing skeleton of a leg that repeatedly impacts the ground is risky**: FR-4 is brittle (low fracture toughness), fatigue-cracks copper traces under repeated flex (especially ED copper), absorbs moisture, and delaminates under combined heat + mechanical cycling. **This is a measure-don't-believe zone** — build coupons and fatigue-test them under your actual impact PSD before committing structure to the material.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Documentary + architecture (do first, this week):**
- Delete the 20-H rule and the decade capacitor-ladder from your grimoire's "do" list; add "many identical caps, minimize mounting inductance" and "solid unbroken reference plane, signal one dielectric away" as the master grounding rule.
- Pull and archive the core goblin docs: TI SLUP132/SLUP197/SLUP198/SLUP230, LT AN47/AN25/AN43, ADI AN-1368, Würth Trilogy of Magnetics + REDEXPERT. Clone your motor-driver vendor's eval-board gerbers.
- Decide the driver now: **moteus or ODrive** (or a DRV8323-based custom board) — not a drone ESC. Benchmark: if a joint must hold static torque at zero speed, a six-step ESC is disqualified.

**Stage 2 — Motor + DOE panel:**
- Accept that a JLCPCB-class 2/4/6-layer 1–2 oz stator will deliver a small fraction of the arXiv 48-layer HDI torque; prototype to *learn scaling*, not to hit 158 mN·m. Add series inductors and plan for ≥50 kHz FOC to tame low inductance. Threshold: if measured phase L < ~50 µH, add series L or raise fPWM until current ripple <10% of rated.
- Lay out the 140-board run as a Taguchi/fractional-factorial sweep. Group into panels of ≤5 designs (free) or pay for a ≤10-design V-scored panel; put an impedance coupon and a board-ID strap + QR on every variant; keep a serial-keyed bring-up log.

**Stage 3 — Mechanical qualification (gate before you trust PCB-as-structure):**
- Build FR-4 and flex coupons; measure first natural frequency and fatigue them under your leg-impact PSD. Benchmark: board fn ≥ 2× chassis mode (target higher); use RA copper and IPC-2223 dynamic bend radii (≥100× thickness single-layer) in every flex hinge; corner-bond drivers/connectors per IPC-A-610/NASA-STD-8739; gold connectors; soft-mount the IMU.
- Change-the-plan triggers: if coupons crack traces before your target cycle count, move structural load off FR-4 onto a metal/composite skeleton and keep PCB for hinges/wiring only (the Harvard PC-MEMS division of labor).

## Caveats
- **Fab blogs are rules-of-thumb, not audited data.** JLCPCB/PCBWay/AllPCB numbers (copper density targets, "30% fewer cracks with teardrops," price thresholds, feeder fees) are marketing-adjacent; verify at quote time and by measurement. Panelization, parts-tier rules, and the $1.5 feeder fee change frequently — re-check before your run.
- **The 20-H, split-plane, star-ground, and mounting-hole debates are genuinely disputed** (see below); I have given the measured verdict where measurements exist (20-H) and flagged the rest as context-dependent.
- **The arXiv 2509.23561 numbers are a single prototype from an interested party** (Microbot Motor co-authors) benchmarked against one Maxon motor; treat efficiency/torque-density claims as indicative, not independently replicated. It is also **cored, not coreless** — do not assume coreless JLCPCB stators reach the same flux.
- **IPC-2223 exact bend-radius multipliers and IPC-4562 fatigue grades are behind paywalls**; the 6×/100×/150×/200× multipliers are consensus values quoted by fabricators and should be confirmed against the purchased standard for any flight-critical design.
- **US 11,342,813 is a spacecraft (Blue Canyon) reaction-wheel motor patent**, not a legged-robot patent; the thermal-trace mechanism transfers, but its vacuum context differs from your convective one.
- **PCB-as-primary-structure under repeated impact is the thinnest-evidence part of this report** — the proven work is compliant-hinge / insect-scale (Harvard) or static structure, not rigid FR-4 leg skeletons taking ground-reaction impulses. Measure before you believe.

### Genuinely disputed among experts (strongest case each side)
- **20-H rule:** *For:* near-field containment is real and it can help when combined with edge stitching to a chassis. *Against (stronger, measured):* Shim & Hubing show total radiation rises ≥3.6 dB at the TM10/TM01 modes; IEEE MoM work shows induced common-mode current. Verdict leans debunked.
- **Split/isolated analog-digital ground planes:** *For (Ott, historically, and many ADC vendor app notes):* a moat + single bridge keeps noisy digital return current out of the sensitive analog reference. *Against (Hartley, Archambeault):* a split forces high-speed return currents to detour, increasing loop area and EMI; a single solid plane with careful placement is better above low frequencies. Verdict: solid plane wins for high-speed; splits defensible only for specific low-frequency precision/mixed-signal cases with slow signals.
- **Star ("single-point") ground:** genuine below ~50–100 kHz; harmful at RF. Both camps are right within their frequency regime.
- **Grounded vs isolated mounting holes:** *For grounding all holes:* maximum ESD shunting and shielding when tied to chassis. *For grounding one / isolating the rest:* prevents ground loops and circulating currents; safety/galvanic-isolation requirements (medical, offline supplies) may forbid bridging primary/secondary grounds. Verdict: genuinely application-dependent — a safety and EMC decision, not a universal rule; anyone stating an absolute is overreaching.
