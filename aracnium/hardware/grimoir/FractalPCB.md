# Why PCBs Are Rectangular — And Who Is Deliberately Breaking the Mold

## TL;DR
- PCBs are rectangular mostly for **manufacturing economics and legacy tooling**, not deep physics: standard 18×24-inch fabrication panels, straight-line V-scoring, straight conveyor rails, and EDA tools that assume orthogonal outlines all reward rectangles. Circular and organic shapes are almost always manufacturable — they just cost more in material yield, depaneling method (tab-routing instead of V-score), and CNC routing time.
- **Circular and radial geometry is already standard, not exotic**, wherever the physics is rotationally symmetric: LED ring lights, Qi wireless-charging spiral coils, axial-flux PCB-stator motors (Infinitum, ECM), circular microphone arrays (ReSpeaker/XMOS), rotary encoders, and gyroscope/IMU mounts. Fractal and space-filling geometry genuinely wins in two domains above all — **antennas** (multiband/miniaturization) and **IC-level fractal capacitors** (higher capacitance density).
- The honest verdict: circular/fractal geometry is a **real engineering win only where the underlying field, coil, or wavefront is itself circular, or where space-filling packs electrical length into small volume**. Elsewhere it is neutral-to-worse on cost, routing density, and testability. And the "fractal is magic" narrative is overstated — peer-reviewed work shows self-similarity per se adds nothing beyond space-filling that a plain meander line achieves.

## Key Findings

1. **The rectangle is a manufacturing/economics default, largely challengeable dogma — not physics.** Standard fab panels are 18×24 in (457×610 mm), with usable area ~16×22 in after rails/tooling. Rectangular boards tile these panels with 80–95% material utilization vs. 40–50% for individual processing; irregular shapes drop utilization and force tab-routing. V-scoring (fast, cheap, straight-line only) works only for rectangles; circles require CNC tab-routing/mouse-bites. Conveyor rails need two parallel straight edges with 3–5 mm component clearance, so a circle is run inside a rectangular carrier panel anyway.

2. **Non-rectangular boards cost extra mostly through yield and routing time, not a shape surcharge.** Some fabs (Bittele) explicitly charge nothing extra for shape and quote by bounding box; others charge for irregular outlines and internal slots. The real penalty is (a) lost panel area (a circle inscribed in its bounding square wastes ~21.5% of that square), (b) tab-routing rather than V-score, and (c) CNC time for complex contours.

3. **Circular/radial layouts are already the norm in whole product categories** — LED rings, Qi coils, axial-flux PCB-stator motors, circular mic arrays, encoders, slip rings, and cylindrical avionics/downhole tools. These aren't rebellions against the rectangle; they're cases where the physics is radial and the rectangle would be the awkward choice.

4. **Fractal geometry's strongest real foothold is antennas and IC capacitors** — but the "fractal is uniquely magic" story is disputed. Fractus S.A. genuinely shipped more than 30 million multilevel/fractal antennas and won a $23.1M jury verdict against Samsung; Samavati/Hajimiri fractal capacitors are a real IC technique delivering a measured 2.3× capacitance-density boost. But independent IEEE literature (Best, 2002–2003) shows fractal self-similarity per se gives no advantage over an equal-length meander line.

5. **Non-Manhattan and curved routing is real but niche.** The X-Architecture (Simplex/Cadence) proved 45° IC routing works; modern KiCad/Altium support arc/curved traces. Electrically, curves matter mainly for impedance continuity in RF/high-speed and for flex-fatigue — the "90° corners are antennas" fear is largely a debunked myth below ~1 GHz.

6. **Cheap-prototype fabs and the PCB-art community are actively exploring organic shapes.** JLCPCB ($2 for 5 small boards), PCBWay, and OSH Park ($5/sq-in) make experimentation nearly free; Boldport/Saar Drimer, the badgelife scene, and Mohit Bhoite have turned non-rectangular, organic PCB shapes into an art form.

## Details

### 1. The rectangular default: what's real constraint vs. inherited convention

**Panelization and material yield (real economics).** Bare-board fabs build on standard laminate/fabrication panels — most commonly **18 × 24 inches (457 × 610 mm)**, also 12×18, 16×18, 21×24 and 24×36 in. After border rails, tooling holes, fiducials, and test coupons, the usable area of an 18×24 panel is roughly **16×22 in (406×559 mm)**. Rectangular boards tile this usable area with little waste. JLCPCB's own panelization guide states material utilization rises from "perhaps 40–50% (individual processing) to 80–95% (optimized panelization)," and that FR-4 laminate is "a significant cost driver" — so packing efficiency translates directly to price. Industry practice treats **above 80% panel utilization as good and below 70% as inefficient** (ALLPCB advises using "at least 70–80% of the panel area for actual PCBs"). A rectangle inscribes into the panel grid perfectly; a circle inscribed in its bounding square wastes ~21.5% of that square before you even count inter-board gaps.

**Depaneling method (real constraint that favors rectangles).** The cheapest, fastest separation is **V-scoring** — an angled blade cuts grooves top and bottom, leaving ~1/3 thickness, and boards snap apart. V-scores must be **straight lines spanning the full panel**, so V-scoring "does not work with circular or some complex geometries" (PentaLogix). Non-rectangular boards must instead use **tab-routing with mouse-bites** (a CNC router cuts the outline leaving small perforated tabs) — slower, leaves rougher edges, and needs ~300 mil spacing between non-rectangular boards vs. ~100 mil for tab-routed rectangles (Omni). So the depaneling penalty for circles is real but modest.

**Pick-and-place rails and conveyor clearance (real constraint — but solved by carrier panels).** SMT lines grip boards by **two parallel straight edges** along conveyor rails; the SMEMA standard transport clearance is **0.200 in (5 mm)**, and components must clear the long edges by ~3 mm (top) / 5 mm (bottom). Edge rails are typically **3–5 mm (industry standard 5 mm), up to 5–10 mm**. A circle has no parallel straight edges — but this is trivially solved by running the circular board inside a rectangular breakaway carrier, which is standard practice. So the conveyor is not a reason a *finished product* can't be round; it's a reason the *assembly carrier* is rectangular.

**Test fixtures / bed-of-nails, enclosures, EDA tooling (mixed — some real, some convention).** Bed-of-nails fixtures and standard enclosure/rack systems (Eurocard 100×160 mm, 19-inch rack, etc.) are built around rectangles, reinforcing the default. EDA tools historically assumed orthogonal board outlines and "Manhattan" (H/V) routing; getting curved outlines and arcs was awkward until recently. This is the most "dogma-like" layer: the tools default to rectangles because boards were rectangular, and boards stay rectangular partly because the tools do.

**Verdict on Section 1:** The genuine physics constraint is essentially zero — FR-4 doesn't care about shape. The genuine *economic* constraints are panel yield and V-score-vs-tab-route. The rest (conveyor, enclosures, EDA) is tooling convention that is challengeable and increasingly challenged.

### 2. Where circular and radial boards are already standard

- **Axial-flux motors with PCB stators (the flagship example).** **Infinitum Electric** (Round Rock, TX) replaces the copper-wound iron stator with a **PCB stator** — a flat, radial, spiral-copper disc. Machine Design (Jan 2024) quotes the company that the PCB stator is "10 times more reliable, 10% more efficient, and 50% smaller and lighter," delivering system efficiency "upwards of 93%" and "up to 4–5× the current density of a conventional, liquid-cooled motor," with noise ~5 dB lower; the design is covered by "43 issued patents and 48 pending patents" (BusinessWire, Jan 22, 2024). **ECM (PCB Stator Tech)** licenses the same idea with its **PrintStator** software that auto-generates Gerbers for circular stators, claiming motors "up to 80% lighter." Here the PCB *is* the electromagnetic device, and it must be circular.
- **Qi wireless-power coils.** The Wireless Power Consortium's Qi coils are "typically planar and round," made of litz wire or **spiral copper traces on PCB/flex** over a ferrite plate, for up to 15 W (Würth Elektronik). Mohan/Lee's classic expressions for square/hexagonal/octagonal/circular spiral-inductor inductance underpin the design math. Reported PCB-coil power-transfer efficiency reaches ~85.6%.
- **Circular microphone arrays for beamforming.** Seeed's **ReSpeaker** boards use circular 4-/6-/7-mic arrays (XMOS XVF3800 / XVSM-2000) for 360° far-field voice capture, direction-of-arrival, and beamforming — the 70 mm-diameter 7-mic array mirrors Amazon Echo's 6-mic ring. Circular symmetry gives uniform angular resolution.
- **LED ring lights and circular arrays**, **rotary encoders**, **slip rings**, **gyroscope/IMU mounts**, and **cylindrical avionics/rocket/downhole tools** all use circular PCBs because the mechanical/optical/rotational system is round. Stacked cylindrical avionics and oil-and-gas downhole tools use circular or annular boards to fit tubular housings; capsule endoscopes and catheters use round or flex-wrapped boards for the same reason.

### 3. Fractal geometry in electronics — the strongest thread, honestly assessed

**Fractal antennas — the origin and the two camps.**
- **Nathan Cohen** built "the world's first fractal element antenna" in 1988 (per Fractal Antenna Systems), founded **Fractal Antenna Systems Inc.** (Waltham/Bedford, MA) in 1995, and filed the foundational U.S. Patent 6,452,553 (filed Aug 1995). His patents cover Koch, Minkowski, Sierpinski, Cantor, and other motifs. **Carles Puente-Baliarda** at UPC Barcelona independently published the **Sierpinski gasket multiband monopole** (Electronics Letters, 1996) and the **Koch monopole** (IEEE T-AP, vol. 48, 2000), founding **Fractus S.A.** (1999).
- **Why fractals help:** self-similarity → multiple resonances (multiband); space-filling → long electrical length in small area (miniaturization). A Sierpinski gasket resonates at multiple self-similar bands; a Koch monopole shifts resonance lower as iterations increase.
- **Commercial reality (verified):** **Fractus is the strongest verified case.** Its corporate page states it "has shipped more than 30 million antennas worldwide." It sued ten handset makers in 2009 (*Fractus v. Samsung et al.*, 6:09-cv-203, E.D. Texas). On May 23, 2011 a jury found Samsung **willfully infringed four "Multilevel" patents (US 7,015,868; 7,123,208; 7,397,431; 7,394,432), awarding exactly $23,129,321** — "over 35 cents per phone" per Fractus counsel Susman Godfrey, versus Samsung's argued 1 cent. Judge Leonard Davis added ~$15M for willfulness in 2012 (Fractus's "$41M" release); Samsung settled on appeal in 2014 (undisclosed). Nine of the ten original defendants settled (Fractus states ~$70M total), and licensees include Motorola, LG, HTC, RIM/BlackBerry, ZTE, and ultimately Samsung. So fractal/multilevel antennas genuinely shipped in real phones.
- **The critical caveat (the honest part):** independent IEEE literature disputes that *fractal self-similarity itself* is special. **Steven R. Best** ("On the performance of the Koch fractal and other bent wire monopoles as electrically small antennas," and IEEE Trans. Antennas Propagat. vol. 51, pp. 1292–1300, June 2003) found "the Koch fractal monopole does not exhibit unique behavior as a function of its fractal geometry… the meander line and normal mode helix are shown to exhibit similar or improved performance." Even the most-cited pro-fractal review (Gianvittorio & Rahmat-Samii, IEEE AP Magazine, 2002) attributes the benefit to **space-filling**, not self-similarity. Hansen & Collin's *Small Antenna Handbook* (2011) bluntly concludes fractals "offer no advantage" over fat/loaded dipoles or loops. Cohen's marketing claims ("hundreds of millions of devices," "you… [can't] fly on a commercial jet without fractal antennas") are self-sourced (the IEEE-USA InSight and ETHW profiles derive from Cohen and use company photos) and not independently verified; the company's own releases cite only "tens of thousands" of its antennas. **Bottom line: fractal antennas are real and commercially deployed, but "fractal" is essentially a good space-filling trick, not electromagnetic magic — any meander/space-filling curve of equal electrical length performs about the same.**

**Fractal capacitors (a genuine, quieter win).** Samavati, Hajimiri, Shahani, Nasserbakht & Lee, "Fractal Capacitors," IEEE J. Solid-State Circuits, vol. 33, no. 12, pp. 2035–2041 (Dec. 1998), showed a fractal (Koch-island-style) lateral-flux metal structure that boosts capacitance density, retains linearity, reduces bottom-plate parasitics, and **improves as process geometry scales** — unlike parallel-plate caps. The paper reports "a factor of 2.3 increase in the capacitance per unit area," with the authors projecting "capacitance boost factors in excess of ten may be possible as technology continues to scale." This is a real IC-design technique — lateral-flux/finger ("fractal") capacitors are ubiquitous in RF CMOS today.

**Fractal metamaterials/FSS/cloaks (research + niche defense).** Fractal Antenna Systems demonstrated a fractal-resonator **wideband "invisibility cloak"** at microwave frequencies — a man was cloaked in 2012 (patented, US 10,027,033) — plus cloak-*detection* (US 11,408,976), fractal "license plates" for satellites, and enhanced radar road reflectors. These are real patents and demos but are research/defense-niche, not mass-deployed consumer tech. Fractal frequency-selective surfaces and fractal inductors exist mainly in the literature.

### 4. Space-filling curves and non-Manhattan / curved routing

- **X-Architecture (IC level).** Simplex Solutions launched the **X Initiative** to promote pervasive 45° ("octilinear") routing; **Cadence** acquired Simplex and the X-Architecture (routes metal layers 4–5 diagonally). The SLIP 2002 paper reported X is "superior on virtually every important measure," reducing wirelength and via count — and crucially that "the fundamental reasons [diagonal wiring wasn't used] are not manufacturing limitations… but EDA limitations." Real chips taped out (UMC foundry accepted 0.18/0.15/0.13 µm designs; ATI reported up to 20% area reduction or 23% performance improvement on individual designs). But it never became mainstream — the whole tool flow (floorplanning, placement, routing, extraction) must change, and sub-32nm lithography pushed back toward rectilinear.
- **Curved/arc traces (PCB level).** KiCad added curved-track support around v5.99/v6–v7 (plus community RF-tools plugins for arc bends and teardrops); Altium and others support arcs; TopoR and AI routers (Quilter) offer organic/any-angle routing. **RF engineers' real view:** curves matter for **impedance continuity** on controlled-impedance and differential nets and for **flex-PCB fatigue**, and they eliminate **acid traps** (acute-angle etch pockets that over-etch traces). But the "90° corners radiate / cause reflections" fear is **largely a debunked myth** below multi-GHz — Altium's own testing found right-angle bends caused "no detectable change in impedance even with an edge of 125 picoseconds." Practicing engineers note curved routing often costs far more layout time (it disables push-and-shove) for negligible benefit on ordinary nets: use 45°/arcs on impedance-controlled RF nets, don't fear corners elsewhere.

### 5. The honest engineering verdict

**Where circular/fractal genuinely wins:**
- Circular: when the device is rotationally symmetric (motors, coils, mic arrays, encoders, ring lights) or must fit a round housing (avionics, downhole, capsule endoscopes). Here a rectangle is the worse choice.
- Fractal/space-filling: antennas needing multiband/small size; IC fractal/lateral-flux capacitors; length-matching serpentines. Space-filling packs electrical length into small area — a real, physics-backed win, though not one that requires *fractal* self-similarity specifically.
- Curved traces: impedance continuity on RF/high-speed nets; flex-fatigue resistance; acid-trap elimination.

**Where it's neutral:** aesthetics/PCB art; ordinary digital routing corner style below ~1 GHz.

**Where it's actively worse:** manufacturing cost (yield loss, tab-routing vs. V-score, CNC time); routing density (radial/polar layouts can waste area and complicate autorouting for a fundamentally Cartesian circuit); testability (bed-of-nails and standard fixtures assume rectangles); assembly (needs a rectangular carrier); and EDA effort (curved routing is slow, loses push-and-shove). Forcing a radial layout onto a Cartesian circuit usually wastes board area.

### 6. Community and accessibility

Cheap prototyping has made shape experimentation nearly free. **JLCPCB** advertises "$2 for 5 boards" (up to 100×100 mm; ~$15–20 shipping); **PCBWay** ~$5 for 5; **OSH Park** charges by area (~$5/sq-in for 3 copies, US-made, ENIG purple). Critically, **most cheap fabs do not charge a shape surcharge** — one maker reported ordering 200 boards "with a lot of cuts and curved edges" from JLCPCB cheaply, while OSH Park refused that same job as too complex. This near-zero cost of odd shapes has fueled a **PCB-art / badgelife** movement: **Saar Drimer's Boldport** (the PCBmodE tool, the Boldport Club subscription, and organic pieces like the "Dreamer" art board and the Calrec anniversary amplifier), the DEF CON **badgelife** scene (Twinkle Twinkie, Andrew Sowa, bleeptrack's generative "Picoplanet" layouts, the "Shitty Add-On" standard), and **Mohit Bhoite** (Particle hardware engineer famous for free-form circuit sculptures — brass-wire rather than PCB, but designed in eCAD and emblematic of the non-orthogonal aesthetic). These practitioners routinely use circular, organic, and non-orthogonal shapes purely because they now can.

## Recommendations

- **Default to rectangular for cost-sensitive, high-volume, testable products.** The economics (panel yield, V-score, standard fixtures) are real. Change only with a reason.
- **Go circular/radial when the physics is radial** — motor stators, coils, mic arrays, round housings. Design the *product* round but keep a *rectangular assembly carrier* (breakaway rails) so SMT lines and V-scoring still work. Benchmark: if components sit within 3–5 mm of the edge, add rails.
- **Use space-filling/fractal geometry for antennas and matching, not as a gimmick.** Treat "fractal" as one space-filling option; a meander line of equal electrical length usually performs the same — choose by fit and fabrication, not mystique. If a vendor sells "fractal" as inherently superior, be skeptical and ask for a head-to-head against a meander of equal electrical length.
- **Use 45° or arc routing on impedance-controlled RF/high-speed and flex nets; don't fear 90° corners on ordinary digital nets below ~1 GHz.** Reserve curved-routing effort for where impedance continuity or flex fatigue actually matters, and use teardrops/45° pad entries to avoid acid traps at pads.
- **Exploit cheap fabs for shape experimentation.** JLCPCB/PCBWay/OSH Park rarely surcharge for outline; prototype freely. Confirm the fab's inside-corner radius (Omni cites a smallest standard inside-corner radius of ~0.016 in / 0.4 mm) and tab-routing rules before committing.
- **Thresholds that change the calculus:** if panel utilization drops below ~70%, or V-score becomes impossible (forcing tab-routing on thousands of boards), the cost penalty becomes material — revisit whether the shape is worth it. For antennas, if you can hit the band/size with a plain meander, skip the fractal.

## Caveats
- **Fractal antenna marketing is inflated.** Fractus's litigation record ($23.1M verdict, grown to ~$41M, 2014 settlement) is hard, court-verified fact; Cohen's "hundreds of millions of devices"/"commercial jet" claims are self-sourced and unverified. Peer-reviewed consensus (Best, 2002–2003; Hansen & Collin, 2011) is that self-similarity adds nothing beyond space-filling. No independent teardown in these sources names a *specific* consumer handset model containing a fractal antenna — the evidence is patent-claim mapping and licensing.
- **"No shape surcharge" varies by fab.** Some charge for irregular outlines/slots; others (Bittele) quote by bounding box. Always check the specific fab's design rules.
- **Fractal metamaterials/cloaks are real patents but niche.** Do not confuse defense/research demos with mass deployment.
- **Curved-trace benefits are net-specific.** They matter for controlled-impedance/RF/flex; for most nets the benefit is aesthetic. The 90°-corner danger is largely historical, tied to older wet-etch processes.
- Several cost/utilization figures come from fab marketing content (JLCPCB, ALLPCB, PCBWay, etc.) and should be read as industry rules-of-thumb rather than audited data. The X-Architecture area/performance figures are vendor/design-team claims from the early 2000s.
