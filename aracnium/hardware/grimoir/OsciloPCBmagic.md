# PCB Magic at the Pinnacle: A Grand-Finale Survey of Oscilloscope RF/Analog Engineering for PCBIUM v3

## TL;DR
- The world's fastest real-time oscilloscopes — Keysight Infiniium UXR at 110 GHz, Teledyne LeCroy LabMaster 10 Zi-A at 100 GHz, and Tektronix DPO70000SX at 70 GHz — are won or lost in three coupled domains: proprietary compound-semiconductor front-end silicon (InP/SiGe), sampling architecture (true time-interleave vs. frequency-band interleaving), and the physical PCB/interconnect craft that carries multi-GHz energy from a precision connector to a bare die with minimal loss.
- The recurring "magic" is geometric and material: keep the coaxial/controlled-impedance environment intact as deep into the instrument as physically possible (chip packages with built-in coax, thin-film multi-chip modules, chip-on-tip probes), aggressively manage the return path and via stubs (back-drilling, stubless blind/buried vias), and correct the residual physics in DSP (S-parameter de-embedding, pole/zero magnitude and group-delay equalization).
- For PCBIUM v3 these instruments validate the project's core theses: controlled impedance *is* geometry, the signal and its return path are one object, "no fake curves" honors the fact that every discontinuity is a resonator, and multilayer layer-assignment is a first-class engineering decision — the titans spend 20–64 layers, exotic laminates, and shielded compartments precisely to make the geometry honest.

## Key Findings

1. **The bandwidth race is a "Moore's Law of scope bandwidth."** Teledyne LeCroy's Pupalaikis et al. ("Technologies for Very High Bandwidth Real-time Oscilloscopes") documented "an average yearly increase in bandwidth of approximately 28 percent which amounts to a doubling of bandwidth every 2.8 years… this trend has been fairly constant for the last thirty five years." The current ceiling for real-time scopes is 110 GHz (Keysight UXR) and 100 GHz (LeCroy LabMaster 10-100Zi-A, via interleaving). Notably, the paper's own theory is that this progression "is driven by testing needs as opposed to being enabled by chip technology."
2. **Two philosophies split the field.** Keysight's UXR reaches >40 GHz *without* frequency interleaving using true full-bandwidth time-interleaved sampling (TIS) on proprietary InP; Tektronix (ATI), Teledyne LeCroy (DBI), and Keysight's own legacy "RealEdge" reach high bandwidth by splitting the input into frequency bands, digitizing each, and stitching in DSP. Each approach carries a distinct noise/complexity trade.
3. **Custom silicon is non-negotiable.** Every titan designs its own front-end ASICs/MMICs. As Tektronix's Mark Briscoe put it about front-end silicon, "There are only two or three companies in the world that can do this, and we had to build our own." The players: Keysight (proprietary InP + GaAs fab), Tektronix (Tek049/Tek079 ADC, Tek085 preamp), Teledyne LeCroy (SiGe MSH/MAD/MAM/MTT chip family plus Teledyne Scientific InP), and Rohde & Schwarz (in-house acquisition/processing ASICs).
4. **The interconnect is treated as sacred.** LeCroy's sample/hold chip package has a built-in coaxial connector so cables from the scope's front bulkhead land directly on the chip package; Keysight builds InP MMICs into thin-film multi-chip modules inside a Faraday cage; Tektronix and Keysight put an active amplifier on the probe tip millimeters from the DUT.
5. **PCBs are extreme.** Flagship acquisition boards are very thick, high-layer-count controlled-impedance stacks (the UXR acquisition board was estimated at ~32 layers in teardown), with back-drilled or stubless vias, low-loss laminates, and compartmentalized shielding.
6. **Physics you cannot build out, you correct.** Real-time DSP de-embedding (S-parameters), magnitude/phase equalization, and interleave-mismatch calibration are as central as the analog path.

## Details

### 1. The Titans and Their Flagships

**Keysight Technologies (formerly Agilent/HP) — Infiniium UXR-Series.** The UXR is the current apex of single-instrument real-time bandwidth: up to 110 GHz of true analog bandwidth on up to four simultaneous channels, 256 GSa/s per channel, and a full 10-bit ADC — the first high-performance scope with 10-bit resolution across the board. Keysight quotes noise as low as 210 µVrms at 10 mV/division, 25 fs rms intrinsic jitter, <10 fs inter-channel jitter, and up to 6.8 effective bits at 13 GHz. Connector strategy tracks bandwidth: 3.5 mm (≤33 GHz), 1.85 mm (40–70 GHz), and ruggedized 1 mm (80–110 GHz). A four-channel 110 GHz UXR captures over 10 Tb/s; Hackaday titled its coverage "Tearing Into A $1.3 Million Oscilloscope," reflecting the flagship four-channel MSRP. Its architecture is documented in Keysight's "Journey of a Signal" and the UXR teardown by Shahriar Shahramian (The Signal Path, TSP #133).

**Teledyne LeCroy — LabMaster 10 Zi-A / WaveMaster / WavePro.** The modular LabMaster 10 Zi-A reaches 100 GHz at 240 GS/s (one channel) or 65 GHz at 160 GS/s (two channels) per acquisition module, with ChannelSync architecture matching channels to ~130 fs and intrinsic sample-clock jitter around 50 fs rms. Up to 20 acquisition modules can be combined for very high channel counts (e.g., 40 channels at 65 GHz, or 80 channels at 36 GHz). Below the flagship, the WavePro HD and WaveRunner 8000HD embody LeCroy's "HD4096" 12-bit philosophy: 12 bits all the time at full bandwidth (8 GHz WavePro HD; 2 GHz, 8-channel WaveRunner 8000HD), rather than dropping a 12-bit ADC into an 8-bit signal path.

**Tektronix — DPO70000SX and the new 7 Series.** The DPO70000SX reaches 70 GHz and 200 GS/s on one channel using patented Asynchronous Time Interleaving (ATI). In September 2025 Tektronix announced its first new performance platform in nearly two decades — described by All About Circuits as "the company's first fresh high-performance platform since the 70000 series launched nearly two decades ago." The 7 Series DPO spans 8/10/13/16/20/25 GHz, with a 10-bit ADC sampling up to 125 GS/s on all four channels, up to 2 Gpoints of record length, and 60–70 fs intrinsic jitter. It delivers 7.5 effective bits at 8 GHz falling to 6.5 bits at 25 GHz, built on two new proprietary ASICs (Tek079 ADC, Tek085 preamp), a straight-preamp (not track-and-hold) topology, a "QuietChannel" front-end using continuous-time linear equalization, and a Stratix 10 FPGA feeding a GPU backplane.

**Rohde & Schwarz — RTP / RTO.** The R&S RTP high-performance scope spans 4–16 GHz (16 GHz on two channels, 8 GHz on four), 40 GSa/s, built around in-house acquisition and processing ASICs achieving 750,000 waveforms/s, up to 3 Gpoints memory, a digital trigger to full bandwidth, and — uniquely — real-time hardware de-embedding using user-supplied S-parameters. R&S emphasizes that it has the in-house expertise to design its own analog ICs.

**Yokogawa** rounds out the field with its DLM/DL series in the mid-bandwidth space, but the >30 GHz frontier belongs to Keysight, Tektronix, and Teledyne LeCroy.

### 2. Front-End Architecture and Proprietary Silicon

The front end of a high-end scope is a dual-purpose variable-gain amplifier plus track-and-hold, deliberately placed *with the amplifier* rather than at the ADC. As Pupalaikis explains, putting the track-and-hold in the amplifier section reduces downstream bandwidth requirements to roughly twice the sample rate, easing signal-path matching across the many interleaved ADCs.

- **Keysight InP.** The UXR front-end module is a proprietary Indium Phosphide chipset — a 110 GHz preamplifier and a 256 GSa/s sampler realized as InP MMICs in a thin-film multi-chip module, wrapped in a Faraday cage. Signal path: connector → mechanical attenuator → InP preamp → split to InP sampler and trigger comparator → array of 10-bit ADCs (each 64 GSa/s). Per Keysight's Infiniium UXR-Series Technical Overview, "A single 110 GHz 4-channel Infiniium UXR incorporates over 80 custom Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs), 13 Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs), 9 unique Monolithic Microwave Integrated Circuits (MMICs) and 38 thin films," with MMICs from its own InP/GaAs fab. The teardown identified InP, SiGe BiCMOS, 65 nm CMOS, and 28 nm CMOS nodes across the instrument.
- **Teledyne LeCroy SiGe + InP.** LeCroy's chip family (all prefixed "M" for monolithic): MSH (monolithic sample/hold, IBM 8HP SiGe, 18 dB gain, sixteen 5 GS/s tracked-and-held outputs), MAD (monolithic ADC, IBM 7HP SiGe, 40 GS/s = eight interleaved 5 GS/s ADCs), MAM (monolithic acquisition memory, 16 M samples), and MTT (trigger+timebase, IBM 5HP SiGe). After joining Teledyne in 2012, LeCroy began using Teledyne Scientific's 500 nm InP HBT process (devices with 350 GHz fT, 5 V BVcbo, low-loss BCB inter-layer dielectric, four metal layers) for a DC–65 GHz variable-gain front-end amplifier (gm stage + LNA + dual emitter-follower output driving 50 Ω to 500 mVpp differential).
- **Tektronix.** Historically Tek ran its own IC fab (the Integrated Circuits Operation, sold to Maxim in 1994 but kept on the Beaverton campus). Modern custom parts include the Tek049 (40 nm RF CMOS, 1927-pin BGA, integrating four 12-bit ADCs, memory interface, trigger, DSP) powering the 5 Series MSO, and the new Tek079 ADC and Tek085 preamp in the 7 Series. Tek's Pinpoint trigger has long used SiGe to keep trigger features useful near full bandwidth.
- **Rohde & Schwarz** designs its own front-end and acquisition/processing ASICs in-house, calling them the core enabler of the RTP's flat frequency response and high spurious-free dynamic range.

### 3. High-Speed Sampling and Interleaving

- **Time-interleaved sampling (TIS).** Multiple sub-ADCs sample staggered in phase to multiply effective sample rate. Mismatches — offset, gain, timing/skew, bandwidth — create spurs at multiples of fs/M and image tones. Titans use calibration circuitry and DSP: offset spurs appear at kfs/M regardless of input; gain and timing spurs are orthogonal and estimated by cross-correlating adjacent sub-ADCs; notch filters guard against divergence when a tone lands on a spur frequency. This is the subject of numerous patents (e.g., LeCroy's M-channel offset/gain/phase calibration patents).
- **Keysight full-bandwidth TIS.** The UXR's 256 GSa/s InP sampler "slows" the signal via time-interleave carefully governed by the oscillator board, feeding an array of 64 GSa/s 10-bit ADCs — no frequency interleaving, hence lower added noise/distortion above 40 GHz. Keysight frames this as a return to "industry-proven, low noise, high signal integrity time-interleaved sampling (TIS)" now made possible above 40 GHz by InP.
- **Tektronix Asynchronous Time Interleaving (ATI).** A pre-sampler acts as a harmonic mixer running asynchronously (e.g., a 75 GHz clock for a 70 GHz system), intentionally sub-sampling to fold the upper spectral half into the ADC's Nyquist band; both digitizing paths receive all the signal energy symmetrically, so combining them averages random noise down — an inherent noise advantage over asymmetric band-splitting. The paths are deliberately symmetric in propagation delay and phase.
- **Teledyne LeCroy Digital Bandwidth Interleaving (DBI).** Invented by Pupalaikis, DBI uses a diplexer/multiplexer to split the input into frequency bands (e.g., DC–35 GHz LF, 35–66.5 GHz MF, 66.5–100 GHz HF), down-converts the upper bands with high-side LOs (67.5 GHz, 102.5 GHz — the band is spectrally flipped), digitizes each with an 80 GS/s channel, then upsamples to 240 GS/s, digitally regenerates the LO, up-converts, image-rejects, and sums with crossover-phase correction. Ovenized multipliers and injected calibration tones (20.5/41/82 GHz) track LO phase drift over temperature. DBI, per LeCroy, is the only technique that increases both bandwidth and sample rate; it has been used across generations from the 18 GHz/60 GS/s and 30 GHz/80 GS/s systems up to the 100 GHz/240 GS/s LabMaster.

### 4. PCB Materials and Stackup

While vendors do not publish the exact laminate of their acquisition boards, the engineering principles and the visible construction are well established:

- **Very thick, high-layer-count controlled-impedance stacks.** The UXR acquisition board was estimated at approximately 32 layers in teardown (a hedged visual estimate from the Signal Path video relayed by Hackaday, not a Keysight-published spec). Comparable high-speed practice runs to 20–64 layer ultra-low-loss stacks for 100G/400G-class work.
- **Low-loss/low-Dk/low-Df laminates.** Per PCBsync's RO4350B analysis, "At 10 GHz, standard FR-4 burns roughly 0.8 dB per inch. Rogers RO4350B burns about 0.18 dB per inch" — a ~4× improvement (RO4350B Dk 3.48±0.05, Df 0.0037 at 10 GHz) that motivates PTFE/ceramic-filled and hydrocarbon-ceramic laminates (Rogers RO4000/RO3000 series), and ultra-low-loss glass-reinforced systems (Panasonic Megtron 6/7, Isola Tachyon) with spread-glass fabrics to reduce fiber-weave skew on 56G/112G PAM4 lanes.
- **Copper roughness matters.** Per PCBsync, "At 24 GHz, going from standard ED copper (Ra around 2 μm) to VLP copper (Ra around 0.5 μm) can drop insertion loss by 25%."
- **Via stubs are resonators.** Above ~10 GHz back-drilling (controlled-depth drilling to remove the unused barrel) becomes mandatory (leave ~8–10 mil stub-to-pad clearance); alternatively, mechanically drilled blind/buried vias in sub-laminations, or laser-drilled microvias, give stubless transitions. Ground-via fences, anti-pad optimization, and reference-plane continuity manage the discontinuity.
- **Hybrid stackups.** RF layers on Rogers over FR-4/Megtron for digital and power, mirror-symmetric to prevent warp, with careful CTE matching (Rogers X/Y ~10–12 ppm/°C vs FR-4 ~14–16 ppm/°C) and prepreg/lamination control to hold impedance within ~±5%.

### 5. Signal Integrity and Controlled Impedance

This is the domain PCBIUM most directly models, and the authorities are explicit:

- **Eric Bogatin (Teledyne LeCroy SI Evangelist; Dean of the LeCroy Signal Integrity Academy):** always think in terms of signal *and return path*, never "signal and ground"; return current follows the path of least inductance on the nearest reference plane; discontinuities occur when the return path switches reference planes; tightly vs. loosely coupled differential pairs are a deliberate design choice; a gap in the return path under a differential pair is a measurable impedance discontinuity.
- **Howard Johnson & Martin Graham ("High-Speed Digital Design: A Handbook of Black Magic"):** the "knee frequency" ties rise time to the bandwidth that actually matters; most digital signal energy sits below it; probe ground-loop self-inductance and spurious pickup dominate measurement error; skin-effect and dielectric loss jointly shape the edge.
- **In the instruments:** transmission lines are microstrip, stripline, and grounded coplanar waveguide; differential pairs are length/skew-matched; crosstalk is managed by spacing, guard traces/via fences, and stripline burial; power/ground planes provide plane capacitance and clean return. Residual insertion loss and reflections are corrected by de-embedding and equalization (Keysight InfiniiSim/PrecisionProbe, R&S real-time hardware de-embedding, LeCroy DSP compensation). LeCroy's DSP uses up to 500-pole/zero IIR filters for magnitude correction and all-pass filters for group-delay/phase correction, yielding a near-Bessel response with ~4 ps rise time at 100 GHz.

### 6. Packaging and Interconnect Magic

- **Chip package with built-in coax (Teledyne LeCroy).** Per Pupalaikis et al.: "The package for the MSH has a built in coaxial connector so that coaxial cables from the outside of the scope are connected directly to the chip package, maintaining a high bandwidth coaxial environment for as long as possible." The MSH takes its input from the center of the die and fans tracked-and-held outputs to the periphery.
- **Thin-film multi-chip modules (Keysight).** InP MMICs are integrated into thin-film MCMs (38 thin films in a 4-channel UXR) inside a Faraday cage; industry practice for such mmWave modules is bare-die MMICs on ceramic thin-film substrates (alumina/AlN) with gold wire/ribbon bonds and microstrip interconnect, though Keysight does not publish the UXR substrate material or bond method.
- **Chip-on-the-tip probes (Tektronix, Keysight).** The Tektronix P7700/TDP7700 TriMode probes place an active buffer amplifier (IBM SiGe 8HP) on the solder tip within ~3.2–4 mm of the DUT via the TekFlex connector, minimizing loss, capacitance, and added noise (100 kΩ DC / 0.4 pF AC loading). Keysight's InfiniiMax III uses a proprietary 200 GHz fT InP IC process with backside ground vias and thick-film technology and ~32 fF differential input capacitance; InfiniiMax 4 reaches up to 52 GHz.
- **Thermal management.** InP/SiGe bipolar front ends self-heat, producing "thermal tails" that distort the step response over ~1 µs; large device sizing, careful biasing, and thermal design mitigate this.

### 7. EMI, Shielding, and Mechanical Integration

Sensitive analog paths are protected by Faraday-cage enclosures around the front-end module (explicit in the UXR), compartmentalized shielding cans that isolate noisy from quiet blocks, EMI gaskets, and mechanical structures that double as chassis and heat sink. The shield is not just a metal box — it is a controlled boundary that defines where return currents flow and where fields may couple, essential in a mixed-signal instrument where fast digital, clocks, and switching supplies sit near a µV-noise-floor front end.

### 8. Probes

High-bandwidth probing is a miniature version of the whole problem: minimize loading, put gain at the tip, keep the interconnect short and controlled, and DSP-correct the response using measured S-parameters loaded into the probe amplifier. Keysight corrects InfiniiMax probes to a flat magnitude/phase response, typically to around the 3 dB non-corrected bandwidth to avoid amplifying noise. The InfiniiMax 4 adds a Bessel-Thomson filter for IEEE 802.3ck 53 GBd PAM4 work; ZIF and browser tips trade hands-free soldering against roving flexibility.

## Connections to PCBIUM v3 Themes

- **Controlled impedance as geometry.** The titans prove that impedance is set by cross-sectional geometry and material Dk — trace width/spacing, dielectric height, copper roughness. PCBIUM's discrete points-and-lines on a triangulated C60 sphere can honor this by making impedance a computed property of local geometry + assigned layer material, not a decoration.
- **Signal and return path as one object.** Bogatin's central teaching maps directly onto a discrete-geometry tool: every routed line should carry an explicit return path on an adjacent shell, and crossing a "split" (a missing return face) should register as a discontinuity/resonator.
- **"No fake curves" = every discontinuity is a resonator.** Via stubs, stubs off matched lines, and un-back-drilled barrels are literal resonant elements above 10 GHz. A tool that forbids fake curves is philosophically aligned with the instruments' back-drilling and stubless-via discipline: geometry must be physically buildable and honest.
- **Multilayer shells and layer assignment.** The 20–64-layer hybrid stacks, buried/blind vias, and compartmentalized shells of a flagship scope are exactly the "multilayer shell" abstraction — layer assignment (which shell carries signal, which is a reference plane, which is a shield) is a first-class engineering decision with impedance, crosstalk, and EMI consequences.
- **"Physics is resonant geometry."** DBI's ovenized LOs, the pole/zero equalizers, the knee frequency, and back-drill resonances all say the same thing: the board's geometry and materials set a resonant/dispersive response, and good design either shapes that geometry or corrects the residual in DSP. PCBIUM can surface this by treating each routed shell-path as having a computable frequency response.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Encode the geometry-is-impedance principle (do first).** Make characteristic impedance a live, computed function of local discrete geometry (line width, adjacent-shell spacing) and per-shell material (Dk/Df), displayed continuously. Benchmark: a user routing on the buckyball sees impedance change as they move a line between shells. Threshold to advance: impedance computation matches a 2D field-solver approximation within ~5–10%.

**Stage 2 — Make the return path a first-class object.** Every signal line gets an explicit return reference on an adjacent shell; flag return-path discontinuities (crossing a split, switching reference shells) as resonators. Benchmark: the tool refuses or warns on a differential pair whose return face is interrupted. This directly operationalizes Bogatin.

**Stage 3 — Model vias and stubs as resonators ("no fake curves," enforced).** Represent inter-shell transitions as real geometric objects with stub length; above a user-set frequency knee, surface stub resonance and suggest back-drill/blind-via equivalents. Benchmark: a via stub produces a visible insertion-loss notch in the path's computed response.

**Stage 4 — Add a per-path frequency response and simple de-embedding view.** Give each routed path a computed magnitude/group-delay response (honoring skin effect, dielectric loss, roughness) and let users see the "raw vs. corrected" contrast that LeCroy/Keysight/R&S achieve in hardware. Benchmark: rise-time/knee-frequency estimates track Johnson's formulas.

**Stage 5 — Honor the titans explicitly.** Include named "reference stackups/architectures" as educational presets: a UXR-style shielded thin-film front-end shell set; a DBI-style band-split; a LeCroy coax-to-package direct interconnect. Change trigger: if users want manufacturability realism, add CTE/lamination and layer-count cost models next.

## Caveats
- **Vendor marketing vs. verified fact.** Much front-end detail comes from manufacturer white papers and datasheets (Keysight, Tektronix, Teledyne LeCroy, R&S), which are authoritative on their own products but promotional in tone; noise/jitter/ENOB numbers are best-case specifications.
- **The "~32-layer" UXR board is an estimate.** It originates as a hedged ("perhaps") visual estimate in Hackaday's write-up of the Signal Path teardown video, not a Keysight-published spec. No flagship-scope acquisition-board laminate (Rogers/Megtron/PTFE) is publicly named by any vendor; the ceramic/thin-film substrate materials cited for MCMs are industry-typical, not UXR-confirmed.
- **Chip-process specifics evolve.** IBM's 5HP/7HP/8HP SiGe processes and node names reflect the 2014-era LeCroy paper; foundry ownership (later GlobalFoundries) and Tek's fab history have changed over time.
- **Interleaving trade-offs are contested marketing ground.** Each vendor argues its architecture (TIS vs. ATI vs. DBI vs. RealEdge) is lowest-noise; independent peer-reviewed head-to-head comparisons are limited, and the "best" approach depends on bandwidth, channel count, and application.
- **The 7 Series is new (Sept 2025).** Its field performance and teardown-level PCB details had not yet been independently verified at the time of writing.
