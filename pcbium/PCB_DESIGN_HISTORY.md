# The Titans of the Board: A History and Inner Workings of PCB Design Software (EDA), From Rubylith to Reinforcement Learning

## TL;DR
- PCB design software evolved in four great waves — hand tape-up on mylar (1950s–70s) → mainframe/minicomputer CAD (Racal-Redac, Computervision, SCICARDS, 1965–1978) → dedicated workstations (the "DMV" trio Daisy/Mentor/Valid, 1981) → PC and cloud democratization (Protel/Altium, OrCAD, EAGLE, KiCad) — and today's frontier of AI/generative layout (Quilter, Cadence Allegro X AI, JITX, DeepPCB).
- Under the hood the software is fundamentally a **graph and geometry engine**: schematic → netlist → a connectivity graph of nets/pins/pads, with placement as energy minimization (force-directed / simulated annealing), routing as grid/graph search descended from **C.Y. Lee's 1961 wave-propagation maze algorithm**, DRC as spatial constraint checking, and impedance as numerical field-solving of Maxwell's equations.
- The field rests on named pioneers — **Paul Eisler** (the printed circuit), **H. Joseph Gerber** (photoplotter and file format), **C.Y. Lee** (maze routing), **Mikami-Tabuchi/Hightower** (line-search routing), the **DMV founders**, **Nick Martin** (Protel/Altium), **John Durbetaki** (OrCAD), **John Cooper & David Chyan** (shape-based SPECCTRA routing), **Jean-Pierre Charras** and the **CERN team incl. Tomasz Wlostowski** (KiCad) — and these are the "Titans" to whom this survey bows, with legend flagged as legend.

## Key Findings

1. **The physical substrate came first, and its inventor was under-rewarded.** Paul Eisler patented the etched-foil printed circuit (British Patent 639,178, applied 1943) but never captured its value. The software industry that would serve it did not appear for another two decades.
2. **Nearly every autorouter is a descendant of one 1961 paper.** C.Y. Lee's "An Algorithm for Path Connections and Its Applications" (IRE Transactions on Electronic Computers, EC-10) formalized breadth-first wave propagation on a grid — guaranteeing the shortest path if one exists. Its weaknesses (memory, speed) drove the entire subsequent lineage: line-search (Mikami-Tabuchi 1968, Hightower 1969), channel routing (left-edge, doglegs), rip-up-and-reroute, and shape-based gridless routing.
3. **The commercial arc is a story of consolidation into three giants.** Of dozens of 1980s CAD/CAE vendors, the survivors consolidated into **Cadence** (Allegro/OrCAD/SPECCTRA), **Siemens EDA** (formerly Mentor Graphics: Xpedition, PADS, HyperLynx), and **Zuken** (CR-8000, CADSTAR) at the high end, with **Altium** dominating the mid-market and **KiCad** the open-source world.
4. **Open source went from hobby project to serious tool via CERN.** KiCad (Jean-Pierre Charras, 1992) was transformed after ~2013 when CERN funded professional features — most famously the push-and-shove interactive router by Tomasz Wlostowski.
5. **The 2025–2026 frontier is "generative" layout, but marketing outruns proof.** Physics/RL-driven tools (Quilter, Allegro X AI) claim order-of-magnitude speedups; a parallel controversy over Google's 2021 RL chip-placement paper is a cautionary tale about extraordinary claims lacking reproducibility.

## Details

### PART 1 — THE FULL TIMELINE: INCEPTION → NOW

#### 1.0 The pre-software substrate (1900s–1950s)
The printed circuit predates any software. German inventor **Albert Hanson** described flat foil conductors laminated to an insulating board in a 1903 patent; **Charles Ducas** patented a stencil/electroplating method (1925); **Arthur Berry** patented a print-and-etch method (1913). But the "father of the PCB" is **Paul Eisler** (1907–1992), a Vienna-trained engineer who fled Nazi persecution to Britain. Around 1936 he conceived the etched-foil circuit while designing a radio; in February 1943 he applied for British Patent 639,178, "Manufacture of Electric Circuits and Circuit Components." Wartime demand — notably the U.S. proximity fuze — drove adoption. In 1948 U.S. authorities ruled that airborne-instrument circuits should be printed. Eisler was commercially under-rewarded: he was on the board of Technograph, which licensed the technology but never won a monopoly, and he was recognized as inventor only in 1971. (Sources broadly agree on the 1943 patent; popular accounts differ on whether the concept dates to 1936 or 1943, and on exact commercialization dates — this survey flags the 1936-concept/1943-patent split as the reconcilable reading.)

**The hand-taped era (1950s–1970s).** Before software, boards were laid out at enlarged scale (typically 4:1 or 2:1) using red-and-blue tape and pads on gridded mylar/vellum ("rubylith"/"red-and-blue line" tape-up). A precision photographic camera then reduced this to the 1:1 negative film used to make the board. An experienced designer could tape roughly two hours per equivalent 14-pin IC; pads were ~70 mils, traces/spaces ~25 mils, boards usually double-sided.

**H. Joseph Gerber and the photoplotter (1960s).** The bridge from hand artwork to machine output was the **photoplotter**, invented by **Heinz Joseph Gerber** (1924–1996), a Holocaust survivor who founded Gerber Scientific Instrument Company in 1948 on the strength of his "Variable Scale." In 1964, at RCA's request, Gerber replaced a plotter's pen with a controlled beam of light, drawing artwork directly onto photographic film. The vector-plotting command language he devised — later standardized as **RS-274-D** and then **RS-274X ("Extended Gerber")** — became the de facto PCB manufacturing file format for over half a century. Gerber held over 600 patents.

#### 1.1 The first computer-aided PCB systems (mid-1960s–1970s)
CAD emerged in the mid-1960s along three lines: digitizer-based, design-automation, and auto-interactive CAD.
- **Racal-Redac (UK)** — founded 1965 as a subsidiary of Racal Electronics — was among the first CAD tool providers. In 1970 it released PDP-15-based tools for PCB schematic and layout, including "Z-Router" (a line-probe router) and "A-Route" (a Lee-maze router) — an early commercial embodiment of both major routing families. Racal-Redac introduced the CADET system in 1982 (billed as the world's first microprocessor-based PCB system) and Visula (UNIX) in 1985. Zuken acquired Racal-Redac in 1994 for $19.5M.
- **Scientific Calculations** (founded 1963) introduced **SCICARDS** in 1974, one of the first dedicated PCB layout programs.
- **Computervision** and **Calma** dominated the early CAD market — Computervision in PCB/mechanical artwork (its CADDS software; most of its first 200 systems went to PCB artwork generation), Calma in IC mask (its GDSII format became the IC layout standard). Both, along with Applicon, later drifted toward mechanical CAD.
- **Telesis** produced the **Allegro** layout system (later acquired by Valid, then Cadence — the ancestor of today's Allegro).
- **Zuken** was founded in Japan in 1976 by **Makoto Kaneko**.

#### 1.2 The workstation era and "DMV" (1981–1991)
The early 1980s brought dedicated EDA companies selling integrated hardware+software workstations. The "Big Three," collectively **DMV**, were all founded around 1981:
- **Mentor Graphics** (1981), founded by ex-Tektronix engineers **Tom Bruggere, Gerry Langeler, and Dave Moffenbeier** in Beaverton/Wilsonville, Oregon. It chose Apollo workstations and raced to demo at the 1982 Design Automation Conference; it turned its first profit and went public in 1984. Its later, over-ambitious "8.0" rewrite nearly sank it; Wally Rhines (from TI) became CEO in 1993.
- **Daisy Systems** (1981), founded by **Aryeh Finegold and David Stamm**. Daisy merged with Cadnetix (1988, becoming "Dazix"), struggled, and filed Chapter 11 in 1990; Intergraph acquired it.
- **Valid Logic Systems** (1981), founded by **L. Curtis Widdoes, Tom McWilliams, and Jeff Rubin**. Valid stayed with proprietary hardware too long; Cadence acquired it in 1991.

**Cadence's formation (1988).** Unlike DMV, Cadence was software-centric and formed by merger: **ECAD** (founded 1982 by Glen Antle, Paul Huang, Ping Chao — maker of the "Dracula" DRC tool) merged with **SDA Systems** (founded 1983; SDA co-founded by James Solomon with Berkeley's Richard Newton and Alberto Sangiovanni-Vincentelli). SDA's Solomon nominated the young **Joe Costello** as CEO; under Costello (1988–1997) Cadence became the largest EDA company, cemented when it absorbed Valid (1991).

#### 1.3 The PC democratization (mid-1980s–2000s)
Cheap PCs put board design on every engineer's desk:
- **Protel** — founded 1985 in Tasmania, Australia, by **Nick Martin**, who saw an opportunity to marry electronics design to the PC. Protel's DOS PCB tool was marketed in the US from 1986 by ACCEL Technologies as **Tango-PCB**. Protel became **Altium** (renamed 2001); **Altium Designer** (2005-onward) became the dominant unified Windows-based mid-market tool. Altium moved HQ to the US (2014), acquired Octopart, Ciiva, and Upverter, launched the cloud platform **Altium 365**, and was itself acquired by **Renesas** in August 2024. (Founder Nick Martin was controversially ousted in 2012.)
- **OrCAD** — founded 1985 by **John Durbetaki, Ken Seymour, and Keith Seymour** in Hillsboro, Oregon ("Oregon + CAD"). Its first product was SDT (Schematic Design Tools) for DOS, born in a basement out of a PC-expansion-chassis side project. OrCAD merged with MicroSim (1998, gaining PSpice) and was acquired by Cadence in 1999; it lives on as Cadence's mainstream/mid-market front end alongside Allegro.
- **PADS** — founded by **Gene Marsh** (Pads Software, 1986); passed through Innoveda to Mentor (2002), now Siemens PADS Professional.
- **P-CAD** — Personal CAD Systems, founded by **Richard Nedbal** (1983).
- **EAGLE** — introduced 1988 by German company **CadSoft** ("Easily Applicable Graphical Layout Editor"). Beloved by hobbyists, it was acquired by Premier Farnell (2009), then by **Autodesk** (2016), folded into Fusion 360 Electronics. Autodesk announced end-of-life for standalone EAGLE (product discontinued as of ~2026), a move that dismayed longtime users and drove many to KiCad.

#### 1.4 The modern professional stack (2020s)
- **Cadence**: Allegro X (with the OrCAD front end); acquired Sigrity (2012) for SI/PI and analysis.
- **Siemens EDA** (formerly Mentor Graphics, acquired by Siemens 2017): **Xpedition** (high-end), **PADS Professional**, **HyperLynx** (SI/PI), plus the **Valor**/ODB++ manufacturing side (Mentor bought Valor in 2010).
- **Zuken**: **CR-8000** (3D, multi-board system-level design) and **CADSTAR**.
- **Altium Designer** + **Altium 365** cloud.
- **Autodesk Fusion** (Electronics), the EAGLE successor.

#### 1.5 Open source
- **KiCad** — created 1992 by **Jean-Pierre Charras**, an electronics professor at IUT de Grenoble, France (the name = "Ki" from a friend's company + "CAD"). It remained largely a solo/educational effort for years. The transformation came from **CERN**: contributions began ~2013 (driven by CERN's open-hardware/knowledge-transfer mission — "KiCad can do to PCB design what the GCC compiler did to software"), giving it the push-and-shove router, better geometry, and differential-pair/length-tuning tools. Lead developer **Wayne Stambaugh** has steered the project; the CERN team included **Tomasz Wlostowski** and **Maciej "Orson" Suminski**. The 4→5→6→7→8/9 releases turned KiCad into a genuinely professional tool; version 8 and later are widely used commercially. CERN released its full 17,000+ symbol/footprint component library under an open license.
- **gEDA** — started 1 April 1998 by **Ales Hvezda** to remedy the lack of free EDA tools for Linux/UNIX (gschem, PCB, gnetlist). Now largely dormant/dead (no active development as of 2025); its spirit continues in the **Lepton EDA** fork.
- **Fritzing** — began August 2007 as a state-funded research project at the Interaction Design Lab, University of Applied Sciences Potsdam, Germany (founders incl. Prof. Reto Wettach, André Knörig). A breadboard-to-PCB tool for makers (popular with Arduino/Raspberry Pi users), it survived a funding crisis (the supporting e.V. ceased operations in 2018) and was revived from 2019.
- **LibrePCB** — founded 2013 by **Urban Bruhin**, written from scratch in C++11/Qt5; first stable release November 2018, version 1.0 in 2023.

#### 1.6 The current frontier (2023–2026)
- **Cloud/browser EDA**: **Flux.ai** (founded 2019, San Francisco, by Matthias Wagner, Lance Cassidy, Christian Blank) is browser-based collaborative EDA with an "AI Copilot"; it raised a $12M seed (2021) and a subsequent 8VC-led Series B. **EasyEDA** (originated ~2010, Shenzhen; integrated into the JLC Group in 2021) is tightly coupled to the **JLCPCB** fab and **LCSC** component catalog — design-to-order in one flow; "EasyEDA Pro" is the newer edition. **Upverter** (founded 2010, Toronto, by Zak Homuth, Stephen Hamer, Michael Woodworth) pioneered fully-cloud collaborative design and was acquired by Altium in 2017.
- **AI/ML and generative layout**:
  - **Quilter** (founded 2019 by ex-SpaceX engineer Sergiy Nesterenko) does fully autonomous placement+routing via physics-driven reinforcement learning, generating multiple validated candidate boards. It announced a **$25M Series B led by Index Ventures on Oct 7, 2025** (bringing total raised to ~$40M, with Benchmark, Coatue, Root Ventures, and Lip-Bu Tan among investors, and Index's Nina Achadjian joining the board); it integrates with Altium/Cadence/Xpedition. CEO Nesterenko frames the thesis bluntly: *"In five years, designing a PCB manually will feel like compiling code by hand."* (a vision statement, not a demonstrated result).
  - **JITX** (founded 2017, Berkeley, by Duncan Haldane, Patrick Li, Jonathan Bachrach, Austin Buchan) is "code-as-hardware": engineers write code (in the Stanza language, designed by co-founder Li — **not** Julia, a common misconception) that a compiler turns into schematics and boards; its autorouter is topological/any-angle.
  - **DeepPCB** by **InstaDeep** — launched November 2019, a no-human-in-the-loop cloud RL autorouter. InstaDeep (founded 2014, Tunis/London) was acquired by BioNTech in 2023: per BioNTech's Jan 10, 2023 press release, *"a total upfront consideration of approximately £362 million in cash and BioNTech shares… InstaDeep shareholders will be eligible to receive additional performance-based future milestone payments up to approximately £200 million"* (total deal valued up to ~£562M / ~$683M).
  - **Cadence Allegro X AI** — announced April 6, 2023. Per Cadence's press release, *"placement and routing (P&R) tasks reduced from days to minutes and with equivalent or higher quality compared with manually designed boards… customers can streamline their system design process achieving a 10X or more reduction in PCB design turnaround time."* (These are Cadence's own marketing claims, scoped to small-to-medium designs, and are not independently benchmarked here.)
  - **Google's RL chip placement** (Nature 2021) is the cautionary counterpoint — see the controversy under Placement below.

### PART 2 — HOW THE SOFTWARE ACTUALLY WORKS UNDER THE HOOD

#### 2.1 The core data model: from schematic to a connectivity graph
The pipeline is: **schematic capture → netlist → footprint/library binding → board database.** 
- **Schematic capture** produces a logical graph of symbols and their pin connections.
- The **netlist** is the crucial abstraction: a list of **nets**, where each net is the set of pins that must be electrically common. This is literally a hypergraph — each net is a hyperedge connecting two or more pins.
- **Component/footprint libraries** bind each logical symbol to a physical **footprint** (land pattern: the pads, courtyard, silkscreen). Standardization here is governed by **IPC-7351** (land pattern standard) and its lineage (IPC-SM-782).
- The **board database** holds geometry (pads, traces, vias, planes, board outline) plus the netlist. Before routing, unrouted connections are shown as **ratsnest / airwires** — straight lines indicating required connectivity, typically drawn as a **minimum spanning tree** over each net's pins (the shortest set of straight connections). The ratsnest is the visual face of the connectivity graph.

#### 2.2 Placement: optimization as energy minimization
Placement decides where components sit. It is an optimization problem minimizing an objective — most commonly **total wirelength** (estimated by half-perimeter bounding box of each net), subject to no-overlap, plus thermal, DFM, and often signal-integrity terms.
- **Force-directed placement** models nets as springs (Hooke's law): connected components attract, overlaps repel, and the system is relaxed to an energy-minimizing equilibrium. Visually elegant and "sim-able."
- **Simulated annealing** (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983) is the workhorse for board-scale placement: random perturbations (swaps, moves, rotations) accepted probabilistically with a cooling schedule, escaping local minima. TimberWolf and VPR are classic SA-based placers; SA has been shown highly effective for the typical component counts of PCBs.
- **Analytical/quadratic placers** (e.g., SimPL, RePlAce) solve a global system then legalize — dominant at VLSI scale.
- **Modern ML approaches**: Reinforcement learning and GPU-accelerated analytical placement (e.g., Cornell's "Cypress" for PCB, 2025). **Caution flag**: Google's 2021 Nature paper claiming RL beats humans/tools at macro placement was disputed. Per Igor Markov's meta-analysis "The False Dawn" (arXiv:2306.09633), *"two separate evaluations filled in the gaps and demonstrated that Google RL lags behind (i) human designers, (ii) a well-known algorithm (Simulated Annealing), and (iii) generally-available commercial software, while being slower; and in a 2023 open research contest, RL methods weren't in top 5."* Andrew Kahng's UCSD team led the reproduction effort; Nature appended an Editor's Note that the performance claims "have been called into question" and retracted the accompanying News & Views; a fired Google whistleblower sued for wrongful termination. The episode is the definitive cautionary tale on AI-EDA hype and reproducibility.

#### 2.3 Routing — the heart of it, and its algorithmic lineage
Routing connects the pins of each net with copper, obeying design rules, across layers. It is **NP-hard** in general and is fundamentally a **search / constraint-satisfaction** problem.

**(a) Maze routing — C.Y. Lee, 1961 (the fountainhead).** Lee's "An Algorithm for Path Connections and Its Applications" (IRE Trans. Electronic Computers, EC-10, pp. 346–365) represents the routing area as a grid and performs **breadth-first wave propagation**: label the source 0, mark all neighbors 1, their neighbors 2, and so on — a wavefront flooding outward — until the target is reached; then **backtrace** along decreasing labels to recover a guaranteed shortest path. Strengths: finds a connection if one exists, and it is minimal. Weaknesses: O(MN) time and memory on an M×N grid — huge for dense boards. This wavefront-and-backtrace is the single most "simulatable," visually beautiful core of the whole field. Antecedent: **E.F. Moore's** "The Shortest Path Through a Maze" (Harvard, 1959). Enhancements: **Akers (1967)** reduced memory via clever label coding (2 bits); **Rubin (1974)** and **Hadlock (1975)** added cost functions/A*-style heuristics to turn the blind BFS into a directed search; **Soukup (1978)** combined depth-first speed with breadth-first completeness.

**(b) Line-search / line-probe (gridless alternative).** To escape Lee's memory cost, **Mikami & Tabuchi** ("A Computer Program for Optimal Routing of Printed Circuit Connectors," IFIP 1968) and **David Hightower** ("A Solution to Line-Routing Problems on the Continuous Plane," DAC 1969) route with **trial lines** rather than grid cells: project lines from source and target, generate perpendicular escape lines where they're blocked, until lines from both ends intersect. Complexity is O(L) in the number of line segments — far less memory. Mikami-Tabuchi (every grid point an escape point) guarantees a path if one exists; Hightower (a single escape point per segment) is faster but can miss a path. Racal-Redac's 1970 "Z-Router" was a commercial line-probe router.

**(c) Channel and switchbox routing.** For structured layouts, routing was decomposed into rectangular **channels**. The **left-edge algorithm** (Hashimoto & Stevens, 1971) assigns horizontal segments to tracks greedily by left endpoint; a **vertical constraint graph (VCG)** captures which nets must be above/below others. **Deutsch (1976)** introduced the **"dogleg"** (splitting a net across tracks to break VCG cycles) and the famous **"Deutsch's difficult example,"** a benchmark of channel density 19 that became the standard stress test — routed optimally in 19 tracks by later greedy/graph routers. LaPaugh (1980) proved dogleg-free channel routing NP-complete.

**(d) Rip-up-and-reroute.** Because greedy sequential routing blocks itself, real routers **rip up** already-placed conflicting traces and reroute them, iterating — the practical answer to routing being a global constraint-satisfaction problem that can't be solved net-by-net.

**(e) Shape-based / gridless routing — Cooper & Chyan / SPECCTRA.** Grid routers waste memory and can't handle mixed trace widths well. **John Cooper and David Chyan** (ex-Mentor) founded **Cooper & Chyan Technology (CCT)** in 1989 and built **SPECCTRA**, the industry's first widely successful **shape-based** autorouter: it represents objects as geometric shapes (not grid cells), enabling different widths, arbitrary clearances, and constraint-driven high-speed routing. Cadence acquired CCT in 1997 for **$418M in stock** (~22.8 million shares) — then the most expensive EDA acquisition ever (the FTC described it as a "$400 million acquisition" and noted CCT was "the only firm with a commercially viable constraint-driven, shape-based router"), largely for the IC-Craftsman router. SPECCTRA became the **Allegro PCB Router**, and its .DSN/.SES interchange files became a de facto autorouter format (also used by the open-source FreeRouting). Alan Finch is separately credited as an early proposer of gridless fine-line PCB routing (1985) and "father of the shape-based autorouter."

**(f) Push-and-shove / interactive routing.** Modern manual routing is "power-assisted": as you draw a trace, existing traces are **shoved aside elastically** in real time while respecting clearances — the "shove" is live constraint-solving. KiCad's celebrated **push-and-shove (P&S) router** was written by **Tomasz Wlostowski** at CERN (initial version September 2013 — his commit message memorably called it "Buggy and crappy"), and is a landmark of open-source EDA. Altium, Allegro, and Xpedition have equivalent interactive engines.

**(g) Topological and any-angle routers.** **TopoR** (Eremex, Russia; roots in a 1988–1996 flexible topological router and the "FreeStyle Router" ~2002) routes **without preferred directions** — any-angle traces and arcs — first solving connection *topology*, then computing geometry, to minimize vias/length and reduce crosstalk from parallel runs.

**(h) The X-Architecture (octilinear/45° routing).** **Simplex Solutions** (CTO **Steven Teig**, president **Aki Fujimura**) introduced the **X Architecture** ~2001, using pervasive diagonal (45°) wiring in addition to Manhattan H/V. Claimed benefits (from the DAC 2002 paper "The X Architecture: Not Your Father's Diagonal Wiring"): >20% wirelength and >30% via reduction. Crucially, the paper stated the reason diagonal wiring hadn't been used before was **not** manufacturing limits but **EDA tool limitations** — routers, extractors, and placers all had to become gridless/non-preferred-direction. Simplex was acquired by Cadence (2002); an "X Initiative" consortium promoted it.

**(i) Modern ML / generative routing.** RL and generative approaches (Quilter, DeepPCB) reframe routing from "route once within rules" to "generate and validate many candidates." See Part 1.6; treat vendor speed claims as self-sourced.

#### 2.4 DRC / ERC, connectivity verification, and copper pours
- **DRC (Design Rule Check)** is spatial constraint checking: verify every geometric relationship (trace-to-trace clearance, min width, annular ring, drill-to-copper, silk-to-pad) against rules — computational geometry over the layout. ECAD's "Dracula" (1983) was the archetypal DRC engine.
- **ERC (Electrical Rule Check)** checks the schematic/logical graph (unconnected pins, output-to-output conflicts, missing power).
- **Connectivity verification** confirms the routed copper matches the netlist graph (no shorts, no opens).
- **Copper pour / plane generation** floods regions with copper for power/ground, computing **thermal reliefs** (spoke connections so pads remain solderable) and **stitching vias** — a mix of polygon/Boolean geometry and clearance offsetting.

#### 2.5 Signal integrity and field solving alongside layout
At high speed, **geometry becomes circuit**: a trace over a reference plane is a transmission line whose **characteristic impedance** depends on trace width, dielectric thickness, and Dk. Controlled-impedance stackups are solved **numerically** by **field solvers**:
- **2D / 2.5D quasi-static solvers** using the **Method of Moments (MoM)** / boundary-element method with layered Green's functions (e.g., Polar Si9000, Simberian's Simbeor in Altium, Allegro's 2.5D solver) mesh the conductor cross-section and solve Maxwell's equations for impedance and propagation. MoM meshes only the metal surfaces (not the dielectric volume), making it fast for planar structures (matrix solve ~O(N³) in surface cells).
- **3D full-wave solvers** (FEM/FDTD; Ansys HFSS, CST) handle vias, bends, and connectors where 2D approximations fail.
This is the same physics idea that motivates a graph/physics simulation: at RF, the "schematic" is an emergent property of the geometry.

#### 2.6 Manufacturing output and the format wars
- **Gerber RS-274-D** (1960s–1980): original vector-plot format, but apertures were defined in a separate file (not standardized) — declared obsolete by Ucamco on 30 May 2014.
- **RS-274X ("Extended Gerber," 1998)**: embedded aperture definitions; became the de facto global standard image format.
- **Excellon**: the companion NC drill file format.
- **ODB++**: an intelligent, database-style single-hierarchy format created by **Valor Computerized Systems** (founded 1992; ODB released ~1995, ODB++ into public domain ~1997). Valor was acquired by Mentor (2010), now Siemens — so ODB++ is often seen as "the Mentor/Siemens format," a point of political friction.
- **IPC-2581** ("the open format" / DPMX): an XML, vendor-neutral, all-in-one format (fab + assembly + netlist in one file), released by IPC in 2004 (built on GenCAM, itself from GenRAD's donated GenCAD). An **IPC-2581 Consortium** formed in 2011 to push adoption as a neutral alternative to the "proprietary" ODB++. 
- **Gerber X2 (2014) / X3 (~2019/2020)**: Ucamco's response — adds attribute metadata (file function, pad function, component/placement data) on top of RS-274X while staying backward-compatible.
The politics: a genuine standards contest between Siemens-owned ODB++ and the IPC-2581 consortium (backed by Cadence, Zuken, Altium, and fabs), with Ucamco's Gerber X2/X3 as the pragmatic incumbent-preserving middle path. Most boards worldwide still ship as RS-274X/X2.
- **Panelization**: arraying multiple board copies (with rails, fiducials, tooling holes, mouse-bites/V-scores) onto a manufacturing panel — a packing/tiling step downstream of design.

### PART 3 — THE TITANS (THE BOW)

To the giants on whose shoulders this field stands — named, with facts, and legend flagged as legend:

- **Paul Eisler (1907–1992)** — invented the etched-foil printed circuit (British Patent 639,178, applied 1943), the physical canvas everything else serves. Honored late (recognized as inventor 1971; IEE Nuffield Silver Medal 1992, shortly before his death), and financially under-rewarded — a recurring injustice in this history.
- **H. Joseph Gerber (1924–1996)** — Holocaust survivor, founder of Gerber Scientific, inventor of the PCB photoplotter (1964) and the Gerber file language (RS-274-D/X) that remains the lingua franca of board fabrication. Over 600 patents.
- **C.Y. Lee** — his 1961 maze algorithm is the intellectual DNA of essentially every autorouter; a genuinely foundational computer-science contribution beyond EDA (robot path planning, networks). Building on **E.F. Moore** (1959).
- **Koichi Mikami & Kinya Tabuchi (1968)** and **David Hightower (1969)** — line-search/line-probe routing, the gridless counterpoint to Lee.
- **The DMV founders** — **Tom Bruggere, Gerry Langeler, Dave Moffenbeier** (Mentor Graphics); **Aryeh Finegold, David Stamm** (Daisy); **L. Curtis Widdoes, Tom McWilliams, Jeff Rubin** (Valid) — who created the dedicated EDA workstation industry in 1981.
- **The Cadence architects** — **James Solomon, Richard Newton, Alberto Sangiovanni-Vincentelli** (SDA); **Glen Antle, Paul Huang, Ping Chao** (ECAD, "Dracula"); and **Joe Costello**, whose leadership (1988–1997) built the modern EDA giant.
- **John Cooper & David Chyan** — shape-based routing (SPECCTRA / IC-Craftsman); Chyan became a Cadence Fellow. **Alan C. Finch** — early gridless fine-line PCB routing (1985), "father of the shape-based autorouter" (d. 2012).
- **Nick Martin** — Protel/Altium; put professional PCB design on the PC and made it affordable.
- **John Durbetaki** (with Ken & Keith Seymour) — OrCAD; schematic capture for the masses.
- **Gene Marsh** (PADS), **Richard Nedbal** (P-CAD), **Makoto Kaneko** (Zuken) — mid-market and international pioneers.
- **Jean-Pierre Charras** — created KiCad (1992) and gave it away; and the **CERN team** — **Javier Serrano** (open-hardware initiator), lead dev **Wayne Stambaugh**, and **Tomasz Wlostowski** & **Maciej "Orson" Suminski** (push-and-shove router) — who turned a teaching tool into a world-class open platform.
- **The unsung** — **Dieter Bergman** (IPC's long-time technical director, land-pattern and data-format standards; the PCB Design Hall of Fame bears his name); the IPC land-pattern standards community (**IPC-7351**, IPC-2221); and the fab/DFM engineers who make the geometry manufacturable.

*Lore flags:* The oft-repeated "diagonal wiring was held back by manufacturing" is explicitly contradicted by the X-Architecture authors (it was EDA-tool limited). AI-EDA "superhuman" claims (Google 2021 RL; some vendor "10X" figures) are disputed or self-sourced. Popular PCB-history pages disagree on whether Eisler's concept dates to 1936 or 1943 — the patent is firmly 1943.

### PART 4 — THE CONCEPTUAL ESSENCE FOR A SIMULATION

The deep, simulatable ideas — the reasons this field is aesthetically and mathematically beautiful:

1. **The board is a graph.** Nets are hyperedges over pins; the whole design is connectivity (topology) plus geometry (embedding). Almost every algorithm is a graph or geometry operation.
2. **The ratsnest is a minimum spanning tree.** Watching airwires resolve is watching an MST over each net — a clean, visual connectivity abstraction.
3. **Routing is wavefront search.** Lee's algorithm is the killer demo: a wavefront floods a grid outward (BFS), hits the target, and backtraces the shortest path — flooding + backtrace, endlessly variable with cost functions (A*, Hadlock, congestion-aware). This is the single most "sim-able" core.
4. **Placement is energy minimization.** Force-directed placement literally relaxes a spring system to equilibrium; simulated annealing "cools" a random configuration toward low wirelength — both are physics simulations you can animate.
5. **Push-and-shove is elastic constraint solving.** Traces behave like elastic bodies parting around obstacles in real time — a satisfying continuous-optimization animation.
6. **DRC is spatial constraint checking.** Computational geometry over the layout: clearances, offsets, Boolean polygon ops.
7. **Impedance is emergent physics.** At RF the geometry *is* the circuit; a field solver turns cross-section shapes into circuit parameters — the bridge from "graph" to "continuous physics."

A compelling simulation could unify these: a live board where you drop components (force-directed relaxation), watch ratsnest MSTs form, launch a Lee wavefront that floods and backtraces to route a net, shove traces elastically, flag DRC violations spatially, and color traces by solved impedance — the whole history of the field, animated as one interactive physics/graph sandbox.

## Recommendations

**For the reader building a physics/graph-based PCB simulation, staged by effort and payoff:**

1. **Start with the wavefront (highest payoff, lowest effort).** Implement Lee's maze router on a grid with animated BFS flood + backtrace. Then layer in cost functions to morph it into A*/Hadlock. This is the most visually arresting, historically resonant core and validates your grid/graph data model. *Benchmark that would change the plan:* if grid memory blows up on realistic board sizes, switch to a line-probe (Mikami-Tabuchi) or shape-based representation.
2. **Add force-directed placement as a spring simulation.** Model nets as springs and components as repelling bodies; animate relaxation to equilibrium. Then offer simulated annealing as an alternative "cooling" mode to show escaping local minima. *Threshold:* if force-directed collapses to overlaps, add a legalization/repulsion term or switch to SA.
3. **Represent the board as a first-class graph** (nets as hyperedges) and render the **ratsnest as a live MST** — this makes the connectivity abstraction tangible and is cheap to compute.
4. **Make routing interactive with push-and-shove.** This is the "wow" feature; study the KiCad P&S router's public design (Wlostowski's FOSDEM 2015 talk "Interactive routing algorithms in modern PCB design tools" is the key primary source, as there is little literature). *Threshold:* if real-time shoving is too costly, fall back to rip-up-and-reroute on commit.
5. **Add a toy 2D field solver** (even a closed-form microstrip/stripline impedance approximation, upgradable to a simple MoM) so users can color traces by impedance — this connects the graph world to the continuous-physics world that clearly excites the reader.
6. **Consider an any-angle/octilinear mode** (à la TopoR/X-Architecture) as a differentiator — topological-first routing then geometry realization is both novel and beautiful.
7. **For an AI angle, be honest.** If you add RL/generative routing, present it as candidate-generation + validation (the Quilter framing), and explicitly avoid the reproducibility pitfalls that sank the Google 2021 claims — log seeds, baselines (always include simulated annealing), and reproducible benchmarks.

## Caveats
- **Secondary-source dates.** Much of the granular company chronology comes from the industry-trade timeline (pcdandf.com) and company/Wikipedia histories; individual dates occasionally conflict across sources (e.g., EasyEDA founding cited as 2010 vs 2014). The Cadence–CCT price is now resolved: **$418M in stock** (~22.8M shares) per Tech Monitor/Semiconductor Engineering, which the FTC rounded to "$400 million"; the earlier "~$420M/~$422M" figures were approximations of the same deal.
- **Marketing vs. verified performance.** Vendor speed/quality claims (Cadence's "10X or more reduction," Quilter's "minutes not weeks," TopoR superiority demos, Flux's "AI Copilot") are self-sourced and not independently benchmarked here; treat as claims, not established facts.
- **The Google RL story is contested and ongoing** — I report the weight of independent evidence (Kahng/UCSD, Markov) as finding the claims unproven, while noting Google maintains its method is in production for TPUs. (Markov, author of "The False Dawn," is now a Distinguished Architect at Synopsys, a Cadence competitor — a relevant, though not disqualifying, affiliation.)
- **Two common misconceptions corrected:** JITX uses the Stanza language, not Julia; DeepPCB launched in 2019, not 2021.
- **Attribution limits.** C.Y. Lee's biographical details are thin in accessible sources; he is universally credited via the 1961 paper but I did not find a reliable full biography. Some founder lists (e.g., Fritzing) vary slightly between sources.
- **Legend flagged, not asserted.** Origin stories that are self-sourced or apocryphal (certain "firsts," marketing-driven AI claims) are labeled as such rather than presented as fact.
